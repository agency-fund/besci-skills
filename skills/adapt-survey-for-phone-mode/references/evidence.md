# Evidence: Move a face-to-face survey to the phone

## Sources

- Dabalen, A., Etang, A., Hoogeveen, J., Mushi, E., Schipper, Y., & von Engelhardt, J. (2016). *Mobile Phone Panel Surveys in Developing Countries: A Practical Guide for Microdata Collection*. World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0904-0 Supports: the call length, simple questions, and the caution about who in a household holds the phone. "As a rule of thumb, interviews during mobile phone surveys are each restricted to about 15–30 minutes, and a typical questionnaire contains around 20 questions"; the authors add that this rests on common practice and "there is no other supporting evidence". "Keeping questions short and simple, preferably without many answer categories that need to be read out, is a safe choice", and long consumption modules "are probably too long for a phone interview". In a trial in Dar es Salaam, IVR "proved clumsy because questions had to be broken down extensively to avoid too many response options". In Tanzania the team began asking the head of household for consent after two reports of women beaten for accepting a survey phone. On airtime payments, the evidence on whether a larger amount keeps more people in a panel is "mixed". Chapters 1, 3, 4, and 5 read in part.
- Gibson, M., & Kopper, S. (2021). *Resources for conducting remote surveys*. Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL). https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/resources-conducting-remote-surveys Supports: steps 1, 4, and 6. "Surveys should be kept to a 30 minute call at most (or multiple 30 minute calls if needed)", and "most surveys should be shortened considerably to only focus on key outcomes". The record of each call attempt should include "the day of the week, time of day, who answered the call, and which language they spoke". On sensitive questions: "eliminate sensitive questions to the extent possible" and "establish clear protocols for asking these questions safely". The page does not cover IVR or whether answers differ by phone.
- Kopper, S., & Parry, K. (2023). *Survey design*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/survey-design Supports: the privacy check. Its section on phone surveys says: "If you do need to ask sensitive questions and the respondent is surrounded by other people, ask if they can be called back later when they are alone."
- Krosnick, J. A., & Presser, S. (2010). Question and questionnaire design. In P. V. Marsden & J. D. Wright (Eds.), *Handbook of Survey Research* (2nd ed., pp. 263–313). Emerald. https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf Supports: the two-step scale. "Oral presentation of 7-point scales on the telephone may require branching, i.e., the conversion of one question into two", and an earlier study found the two-question format "took less time in a telephone survey than the equivalent one-item 7-point scale". The chapter also notes that when options are read aloud, respondents attend most to the last ones heard. The passages on rating scales and on the order of answer choices read.
- Malhotra, N., Krosnick, J. A., & Thomas, R. K. (2009). Optimal design of branching questions to measure bipolar constructs. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 73(2), 304–324. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp023 Supports: asking direction first and strength second. Across several national surveys, a first question on direction, followed by a question on strength for those who chose a side, gave the most accurate scales. Abstract only.
- Greenleaf, A. R., Diakabana, H., & Lau, C. (2026). Computer-assisted mobile phone interviews in low- and middle-income countries through a total survey error framework. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 90(3), 878–910. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfag023 Supports: comparing who is reached with who the survey is about, and simple answer choices. The authors advise: "Even more so than FTF surveys, CATI questionnaires should ask concise questions using simple response options" (FTF is face to face; CATI is a live call with answers entered on a computer). They also report that "a low response rate does not indicate response bias". The first half of the full text read.
- Gourlay, S., Kilic, T., Martuscelli, A., Wollburg, P., & Zezza, A. (2021). Viewpoint: High-frequency phone surveys on COVID-19: Good practices, open questions. *Food Policy*, 105, 102153. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodpol.2021.102153 Supports: treating phone surveys as a complement to face-to-face ones. An overview of design options drawn from national phone surveys run with the World Bank during the pandemic. Abstract only.
- Gyani, A., Tindall, K., & Gallagher, R. (2022). *Explore Field Guide*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Explore-Field-Guide.pdf Supports: choosing the survey format as a named task. Its table "The pros and cons of different survey formats" lists for phone surveys that respondents "can ask for clarification", and that such surveys "are resource intensive" and "response rates for phone surveys are dropping".
- Groves, R. M., Fowler, F. J., Couper, M. P., Lepkowski, J. M., Singer, E., & Tourangeau, R. (2009). *Survey Methodology* (2nd ed.). Wiley. Supports: the topic only. Chapter 5, "Methods of Data Collection", has sections on what the choice of method means for coverage, nonresponse, and measurement quality. Table of contents only.

The privacy check before each sensitive item, the rule to drop an item on a shared phone, and the requirement to name in advance the difference that would change the conclusion are this skill's own.

| [Garlick, Orkin & Quinn (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/lhz021) | Randomized experiment with small enterprises over 12 weeks; abstract only | South Africa | "Few differences" between weekly phone and in-person interviews in averages and distributions. | [Pariyo and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0214450) | Each respondent surveyed by live call and by IVR a week apart; abstract only | Bangladesh, Tanzania | The two modes agreed fairly well on smoking and alcohol and poorly on physical activity, fruit and vegetables, and salt |

These studies disagree with each other on which ages and places are missed and on whether answers differ by phone, so the results beyond wealthy Western countries are recorded as differing.

None found: a test in a low- or middle-income country of a two-step scale against the same scale read out whole, or of how many answer choices respondents can hold on a call. Searches covered reviews of mobile phone surveys in low- and middle-income countries, randomized comparisons of phone and in-person answers, who answers and how private a call is on a shared phone, and two-step scales in phone surveys.

What depends on the place is who owns the phone a number rings, whether a woman can speak on it alone, how good the network is, and how much of the questionnaire is the kind of detailed counting that suffers most on a call.

## Replication

That phone samples miss less educated people has been found in every study here that reported education, and the review also finds poorer people missed; weighting has not reliably repaired it. Who else is missed differs by country and by where the numbers came from: phone surveys of the general population in Nigeria under-represented women, older people, and rural people; panels drawn from national household surveys in Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Uganda reached respondents older than the general adult population; and a follow-up survey in Burkina Faso under-represented 15- to 19-year-olds and urban people. Whether answers differ by phone depends on the question and the study: simple yes-or-no questions held up, weekly enterprise figures in South Africa showed few differences, consumption in Ethiopia came out 23% lower, and contraceptive use in Burkina Faso was 40% by phone against 26% face to face, a gap weighting did not close. The limit on call length is common practice. The scan found no test of the two-step scale in a low- or middle-income country.

## What to check locally

1. Who owns each phone on the list, by sex, and how many respondents are reached through a spouse's or a relative's phone.
2. Whether respondents can take a call alone, and at what times of day.
3. Network coverage and charging in the places the respondents live.
4. Which items are sensitive here, including ones that are not sensitive elsewhere.
5. Whether some face-to-face interviews are possible for the mode check; if not, which earlier face-to-face round asked the same items.
6. Whether airtime can be sent to every kind of number on the list.
