# Evidence: Test whether people understand your survey questions

## Sources

- Willis, G. B., & Artino, A. R. (2013). What do our respondents think we're asking? Using cognitive interviewing to improve medical education surveys. *Journal of Graduate Medical Education*, 5(3), 353–356. https://doi.org/10.4300/JGME-D-13-00154.1 Supports: the shape of the script. The method rests on a four-stage account of answering: "(1) comprehension, (2) retrieval of information, (3) judgment or estimation, and (4) selection of a response", and "Most often, comprehension problems dominate." Thinking aloud "places a significant burden on subjects, many of whom find the process to be unnatural and difficult", while probes "can be targeted toward specific cognitive processes". With an interviewer-read survey "it is common to rely on concurrent probing", after each question; with a self-completed one, probing is often held to the end. Projects "may involve just 10 to 30 total subjects" in rounds, with questions revised between rounds, and the people interviewed "should not be used as part of the final survey study". Read in full.
- Scott, K., Ummer, O., & LeFevre, A. E. (2021). The devil is in the detail: reflections on the value and application of cognitive interviewing to strengthen quantitative surveys in global health. *Health Policy and Planning*, 36(6), 982–995. https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czab048 Supports: probing in place of thinking aloud, which the authors describe as "confusing for many participants, particularly poorer and less educated participants"; working "in pairs (one to lead the interview and one to take detailed notes)"; recruiting those "most likely to struggle with the survey"; allowing "at least 3 minutes" per question; and a grid of questions by interviews for the debrief. The authors place cognitive interviewing after translation and before pilot testing, and say back-translation "does not examine cultural appropriateness or the extent to which questions achieve cognitive match between researchers and respondents". Read in full.
- Conrad, F. G., & Blair, J. (2009). Sources of error in cognitive interviews. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 73(1), 32–55. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp013 Supports: cue probes (conditional probing) only; it does not support the fix rule. Interviewers told to probe only about what respondents had explicitly reported found fewer problems, with higher agreement between judges, than interviewers free to probe at will; probing beyond what respondents had said led interviewers to see problems that most judges did not. Abstract only.
- Blair, J., & Conrad, F. G. (2011). Sample size for cognitive interview pretesting. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 75(4), 636–658. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfr035 Supports: the line that a round does not certify a question. The authors report that "serious problems that were not detected in small samples were consistently observed in larger samples". Abstract only.
- Lenzner, T., Hadler, P., & Neuert, C. (2023). An experimental test of the effectiveness of cognitive interviewing in pretesting questionnaires. *Quality & Quantity*, 57(4), 3199–3217. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-022-01489-4 Supports: retesting every reworded question. Abstract only.
- Martin, S. L., Birhanu, Z., Omotayo, M. O., Kebede, Y., Pelto, G. H., Stoltzfus, R. J., & Dickin, K. L. (2017). "I can't answer what you're asking me. Let me go, please." *Field Methods*, 29(4), 317–332. https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X17703393 Supports: the stop rule and trying the script on two people before the first round. Abstract only.
- Kopper, S., & Parry, K. (2023). *Questionnaire piloting*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/questionnaire-piloting Supports: where and how to test. "Find a population and location similar to what you will use in the study, but do not use your actual study area"; "Conduct the pre-testing in pairs so that one person can engage with the respondent while the other observes and records"; "Ensure field staff read questions as written". The same authors' *Survey design* page (https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/survey-design) lists cognitive interviews among the ways to develop questions. Both read in full.
- Bjärkefur, K., Cardoso de Andrade, L., Daniels, B., & Jones, M. R. (2021). *Development Research in Practice: The DIME Analytics Data Handbook*. World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1694-9 Supports: testing outside the sample. Its content-focused pilot refines "the phrasing and translation of specific questions" and is done "out-of-sample, but in a context as similar as possible to the study sample". Chapter 4 read.
- Gyani, A., Tindall, K., & Gallagher, R. (2022). *Explore Field Guide*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Explore-Field-Guide.pdf Supports: the task itself: "try to pilot your questions by asking people how they would interpret them". Survey section read.
- Krosnick, J. A., & Presser, S. (2010). Question and questionnaire design. In P. V. Marsden & J. D. Wright (Eds.), *Handbook of Survey Research* (2nd ed., pp. 263–313). Emerald. https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf Supports: the limits of the method. Probes are "valuable for revealing respondent interpretations of a question", but "thinking aloud may disrupt" the way people normally answer, and cognitive interviews "have been shown to be less reliable" than coding of interviewer and respondent behavior. Read in full.
- Willis, G. B. (2005). *Cognitive Interviewing: A Tool for Improving Questionnaire Design*. SAGE. https://doi.org/10.4135/9781412983655 The reference text for the method, to which Krosnick and Presser send readers for detail. Cited for the topic only; the text was not read.
- Groves, R. M., Fowler, F. J., Couper, M. P., Lepkowski, J. M., Singer, E., & Tourangeau, R. (2009). *Survey Methodology* (2nd ed.). Wiley. A standard textbook that covers the evaluation of survey questions. Cited for the topic only; the text was not read.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: the fix rule (one problem in two interviews of a round, or once if the respondent could not answer), the default of two rounds of eight, and the list of problem codes.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Scott, Gharai, Sharma, Choudhury, Mishra, Chamberlain & LeFevre (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czz141) | Cognitive interviews with 21 rural women on 88 questions adapted from validated instruments; abstract only | India | Women "tended to provide initial answers", yet the interviews "revealed widespread mismatch between respondent interpretation and question intent" |

| [Wenemark, Ngwili, Ndoboli, Wieland & Roesel (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2022.833748) | Interviews with 12 small-scale farmers; abstract only | Kenya, Uganda | The authors report that "even a small number of interviews and using interviewers with limited knowledge of cognitive interviewing can identify many problems" |
| [Hannan, Heckert, James-Hawkins & Yount (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1111/mcn.12871) | Cognitive interviews with women in three kinds of household in two regions; abstract only | Bangladesh | Questions "were generally understood, but participants occasionally responded to the wrong part of the question, did not understand key phrases, or were uncomfortable with questions" |
| [Lenzner, Hadler & Neuert (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-022-01489-4) | Web survey experiment, 2,200 respondents, after ten cognitive interviews; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | The interviews were "effective in identifying real question problems, but not necessarily in fixing survey questions and improving data quality" |
| [Conrad & Blair (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp013) | Experiment comparing two instructions to interviewers; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Agreement about which answers showed a problem "was surprisingly low overall", yet judges "reached relatively consistent conclusions across the interviews about which questions most needed repair" |
| [Presser & Blair (1994)](https://doi.org/10.2307/270979) | Four pretesting methods applied to one questionnaire in repeated trials; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Expert panels and coding of interview behavior were more consistent across trials than cognitive interviews in the number and kind of problems found |

The scan found no systematic review of cognitive interviewing in low- and middle-income countries, and no study that compares scripted probes with thinking aloud among respondents with little schooling; the preference for probes there rests on the field accounts above. It searched for systematic reviews of cognitive interviewing in low- and middle-income countries, for comparisons of thinking aloud and probing with non-Western respondents, for cognitive interviews on empowerment surveys in Africa and South Asia, and for experiments on whether questions revised after cognitive interviews give better data.

All the field studies above from India, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Uganda found, as the original evidence does, that the interviews exposed misread questions, so the result holds beyond wealthy Western countries. Two of them also found the probes themselves upset or embarrassed some participants, which is why the script has a stop rule and a trial on two people.

What depends on local conditions is how respondents take being probed. Asking a person to explain an answer can feel like an examination where schooling is low or the interviewer has higher status, and that changes which probes can be used.

## Replication

Five field studies in India, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Uganda found that the interviews exposed misread questions. Which problems are found is less stable: individual interviewers and judges often disagree, cognitive interviews are less consistent across repeated trials than some other pretesting methods, and larger samples find serious problems that small ones miss. In the one experiment found, a question reworded after ten interviews did not clearly produce better data than the draft, which is why reworded questions are retested.

## What to check locally

1. Whether respondents are at ease when asked to explain an answer. Try the script on two people and watch for unease before running a round.
2. Whether the interviewers can read the survey question as written and then switch to open conversation; some interviewers find the switch hard.
3. Whether the people recruited match the survey's least-schooled respondents, not the easiest people to reach.
4. Whether a problem lies in the translation or in the idea; someone who reads both languages has to hear the quotes.
