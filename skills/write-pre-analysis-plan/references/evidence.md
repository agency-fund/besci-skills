# Evidence: Write the analysis plan before the data arrive

## Sources

- Duru, M., Sautmann, A., Turitto, J., & Welch, K. (2023). *Pre-analysis plans*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/pre-analysis-plans Supports: what the plan is and when it is filed: it is "filed publicly, typically prior to intervention start but at the latest before data analysis begins"; "A PAP should be concise and focused, rather than attempt to be exhaustive"; "If you find you need to modify your PAP, or you overlooked something, document why you made the edit"; and the populated plan, "a separate document that populates the PAP to the extent possible and discusses any deviations from it". Also the label on anything not pre-specified: such results "should be viewed the same way as those of an observational study that did not have a PAP". Read in full.
- Duru, M., Sautmann, A., Turitto, J., & Welch, K. (2023). *Trial registration*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/trial-registration Supports: step 8. The AEA RCT Registry's mandatory fields include "Primary outcomes, experimental design, randomization method, randomization unit, clustering, sample size" and IRB approval; a plan can be uploaded and "may remain hidden until the trial is complete"; journals that follow the ICMJE recommendations "require pre-registration in an approved clinical trial registry", and "This requirement applies even when the research is within the social sciences". On detail: the entry should be precise for "variables or decisions that require a lot of discretion in their construction and are critical to the analysis, such as primary outcomes or subgroups for heterogeneity analysis". Read in full.
- Banerjee, A., Duflo, E., Finkelstein, A., Katz, L., Olken, B., & Sautmann, A. (2020). *In praise of moderation: Suggestions for the scope and use of pre-analysis plans for RCTs in economics* (NBER Working Paper 26993). https://doi.org/10.3386/w26993 Supports: step 6. "the key benefits of a PAP can usually be realized by completing the registration fields in the AEA RCT Registry"; more detail "may be warranted" where "subgroup analysis is expected to be particularly important, or a party to the study has a vested interest", and where "the same primary outcome could be measured in several different ways". The stake can belong to "an implementing partner, a funder, or an investigator who has advanced a particular hypothesis in prior work". Also "A deviation shown as the result": a new specification should be shown with "the pre-specified analysis alongside", with each clearly labeled. Read in part: the abstract, the benefits and costs of plans, and the questions and answers.
- Brodeur, A., Cook, N. M., Hartley, J. S., & Heyes, A. (2024). Do preregistration and preanalysis plans reduce p-hacking and publication bias? Evidence from 15,992 test statistics and suggestions for improvement. *Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics*, 2(3), 527–561. https://doi.org/10.1086/730455 Supports: writing a plan, not only a registry entry, and "The registry entry as the plan". Preregistration "frequently does not involve a preanalysis plan (PAP), or sufficient detail to constrain meaningfully the actions and decisions of researchers"; the authors find "no evidence that preregistration in itself reduces p-hacking and publication bias", and, with a plan, "evidence consistent with both reduced p-hacking and reduced publication bias". Abstract only.
- Duru, M., & Kopper, S. (2023). *Data analysis*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/data-analysis Supports: steps 4 and 5. Related outcomes can be aggregated "into a single index", or the plan can use a method "controlling the family-wise error rate or false discovery rate", including the Benjamini–Hochberg method; standard errors are clustered "at the level at which treatment was assigned"; stratification variables are included as controls when the probability of treatment varies by stratum; and attrition is checked for "systematic differences in attrition rates between those in the treatment versus those in the control groups". Sections on these topics read.
- Anderson, M. L. (2008). Multiple inference and gender differences in the effects of early intervention: A reevaluation of the Abecedarian, Perry Preschool, and Early Training Projects. *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 103(484), 1481–1495. https://doi.org/10.1198/016214508000000841 Supports: step 4. Combines "summary index tests with familywise error rate and false discovery rate corrections", and concludes it "can be important to declare the family of tests under consideration and to either consolidate measures or report adjusted and unadjusted p values". Abstract only.
- Benjamini, Y., & Hochberg, Y. (1995). Controlling the false discovery rate: A practical and powerful approach to multiple testing. *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B: Statistical Methodology*, 57(1), 289–300. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x Supports: step 4. Controls "the expected proportion of falsely rejected hypotheses", with a gain in power over controlling the familywise error rate. Abstract only.
- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: the task, as "Pre-specify the analysis": "defining the type of regression analysis you will run, including the control variables you might include, and the subgroups you want to look at", and the note that subgroups must be identifiable "with the data you have available". Chapter 4 read in full.
- Olken, B. A. (2015). Promises and perils of pre-analysis plans. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 29(3), 61–80. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.29.3.61 Supports: the replication note. A plan can specify "variables, data cleaning procedures, regression specifications", and if researchers report all pre-specified results "data-mining problems are greatly reduced"; Olken also reviews evidence that "the benefits of using pre-specified analysis plans may not be as high as one might have expected initially". Abstract only.
- Coffman, L. C., & Niederle, M. (2015). Pre-analysis plans have limited upside, especially where replications are feasible. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 29(3), 81–98. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.29.3.81 Supports: the replication note. The authors argue "enthusiasm for pre-analysis plans should be tempered". Abstract only.

Two things are this skill's own and come from no source: the decision rules, which make the plan usable by a program as well as by a journal; and the rule that two primary outcomes are treated as one family with a correction.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026, for systematic reviews and studies of whether pre-registration or pre-analysis plans change what trials report. No systematic review was found. The skill is a procedure and makes no claim about how program participants behave, so this table records where its sources come from and the studies of the practice itself.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Brodeur et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1086/730455) | Study of 15,992 test statistics from trials in 15 leading economics journals; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | No evidence that registration alone reduces p-hacking or publication bias; evidence consistent with reductions when a pre-analysis plan accompanies the registration |
| [Ofosu & Posner (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1537592721000931) | Review of 195 plans from the EGAP and AEA registries, 2011 to 2016, and 93 papers that followed; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | "Significant variation in the extent to which PAPs registered during this period accomplished the goals they were designed to achieve" |
| [Casey, Glennerster & Miguel (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qje027) | Randomized evaluation that used a pre-analysis plan; abstract only | Sierra Leone | The authors show that without the plan they "could have generated two divergent, equally erroneous interpretations of program impacts" |
| [Banerjee et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3386/w26993) | Guidance from researchers and editors; read in part | Written for trials in any country | Argues the registry fields usually suffice, with more detail in three cases |

What depends on the place is practical: which registry a funder, journal, or ethics board requires; whether ethics approval must come before registration; and whether the data structure the plan assumes (IDs that link rounds, cluster identifiers, the baseline variables named as covariates) exists.

## Replication

Contested on how much to fix in advance. Olken (2015) and Coffman and Niederle (2015) set out the costs and argue the benefits may be smaller than expected; Banerjee et al. (2020) argue the registry fields usually suffice; Brodeur et al. (2024) found that registration alone showed no sign of reducing p-hacking while registration with a plan did; and Ofosu and Posner (2023) found that early plans varied widely in whether they were precise enough to do their job. Not contested: whatever is pre-specified must be written before outcome data are seen.

## What to check locally

1. Which registry the funder, journal, or ethics board requires, and whether health outcomes bring in a clinical trial registry.
2. Whether any arm-level tabulation has already circulated, including on a dashboard.
3. Whether the variables named as covariates and subgroups exist in the baseline data, with IDs that link to the outcome data.
4. Which party has the strongest stake in the result, so the plan is detailed where the pressure will be.
