The scan found no meta-analysis of commitment devices with money at stake. What differs by place is whether a regulated holder exists that people trust to return their money, how much of the year a household has anything to spare, and whether relatives' claims on visible cash make a lock welcome.

## Replication

Savings commitment products have been tested repeatedly in low- and middle-income countries, and most raised balances at the bank offering them. Three things did not hold across trials. Take-up did not track self-control problems: of five trials that checked whether people with present bias (a pull toward spending now that they later regret) were more likely to sign up, most found no difference. Opening an account did not mean using it. And devices with a penalty left many committers worse off: 55% defaulted in the Philippine installment trial, and in India people paid for contracts that changed nothing. Outside savings the results split, with a lasting effect on smoking in the Philippines and none on doctor visits in India.

## What to check locally

1. Whether people have the money at some point and lose it to spending or requests, or never have it.
2. Whether the holder of the money is regulated and trusted to give it back.
3. When a lock or a forfeit would fall in a lean season, a fee deadline, or a debt repayment.
4. What people already do to bind themselves, which shows the restriction they accept.
5. Whether a forfeit would be read as a fine from the program.
