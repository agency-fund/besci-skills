# Evidence: Set a realistic effect size to plan for

## Sources

The task is a named step in two process guides:

- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: the check on the central value. Box 1.3, "What impact can you expect from a behavioural intervention?", says that with light-touch interventions "you have to be realistic", and "In BIT's experience, communication interventions often don't bring about a change that is larger than 2-3 percentage points. (However, there are also many exceptions.)" Its example: from 30% of companies paying a tax debt, a realistic goal for a letter is 32%. The guide's Mexico case study, in which SMS reminders went to 748,499 businesses, reports that the most effective message raised on-time declarations from 24% to 33%, and a footnote calls it an effect "much larger than 2 percentage points". Read in full.
- Haynes, L., Service, O., Goldacre, B., & Torgerson, D. (2012). *Test, Learn, Adapt*. Cabinet Office. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7488c8e5274a7f9c586c23/TLA-1906126.pdf Supports: the same rule. Step 4 says it is "important from the outset not to be overly optimistic about the likely success of an intervention. Many interventions - if not most - have relatively small effects." Step 4 read.
- Gupta, S., & Kopper, S. (2021). *Power calculations*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/power-calculations Supports: three rules. "Published studies evaluating similar interventions will typically provide an optimistic estimate of a reasonable effect size"; the difference "will be diluted by any non-compliance, so the expected effect size should be adjusted to the expected compliance rate"; and the planning effect "may be a practically relevant effect size such as the minimum improvement in outcomes required for the implementing partner to determine the program's benefits are worth the costs or to scale the program". It also says Cohen's rules of thumb "are based on social psychology lab experiments among undergraduates" and "have since been argued not to be realistic in real-world settings for some sectors and regions". Read in full.
- Doyle, M.-A., & Feeney, L. (2021). *Quick guide to power calculations*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/quick-guide-power-calculations Supports: separating the smallest effect worth acting on. "The hardest part is choosing a reasonable minimum detectable effect"; for a partner it "might be the smallest effect that would still make it worthwhile to run this program". Read in full.
- OECD (2019). *Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit*. https://doi.org/10.1787/9ea76a8f-en Supports: the same. Box 2.10 says "a better approach when applying BI to public policy may be to assume what effect size will be acceptable for developing a larger policy intervention upon given the expected costs and benefits". Box 2.10 read.

The evidence on how published effects compare with delivered ones:

- DellaVigna, S., & Linos, E. (2022). RCTs to scale: Comprehensive evidence from two nudge units. *Econometrica*, 90(1), 81–116. https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709 Supports: the low end and the one-sixth ratio. The authors compare 126 trials covering 23 million people, all those run by two of the largest US nudge units, with nudge trials published in academic journals. The journal trials averaged 8.7 percentage points, a 33.4% increase over control; the nudge-unit trials averaged 1.4 points, an 8.0% increase. Selective publication, made worse by low statistical power, explains about 70% of the difference; differences in the interventions account for most of the rest. Abstract only.
- Mertens, S., Herberz, M., Hahnel, U. J. J., & Brosch, T. (2022). The effectiveness of nudging: A meta-analysis of choice architecture interventions across behavioral domains. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 119(1), e2107346118. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2107346118 Supports: an example of an uncorrected average. More than 200 studies and more than 450 effect sizes (n = 2,149,683) gave d = 0.45 (95% CI 0.39 to 0.52), with "moderate publication bias". Interventions that change how options are structured outperformed those that describe options or reinforce intentions. Location was coded only as inside or outside the United States. Abstract read; the full text searched for how location was coded.
- Maier, M., Bartoš, F., Stanley, T. D., Shanks, D. R., Harris, A. J. L., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2022). No evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 119(31), e2200300119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200300119 Supports: using the bias-corrected figure, and not pooling across kinds of intervention. Re-analyzing the same data, a random-effects average of 0.43 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.48) became 0.04 (95% credible interval 0.00 to 0.14) after correction, or 0.11 using only each study's most precise estimate. Information interventions fell to 0.00 and structure interventions to 0.12 (0.00 to 0.43); the evidence was against an effect for information and assistance interventions and undecided for structure. All categories and domains except finance showed heterogeneity, so some nudges may work even where the average does not. Read in full.
- Szaszi, B., Higney, A., Charlton, A., Gelman, A., Ziano, I., Aczel, B., Goldstein, D. G., Yeager, D. S., & Tipton, E. (2022). No reason to expect large and consistent effects of nudge interventions. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 119(31), e2200732119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200732119 Supports: the replication note. Mertens et al.'s own adjustment gave d = 0.08 under severe and 0.31 under moderate publication bias; three further corrections gave −0.01, 0.07, and 0.08. The authors see "no reason to expect large and consistent effects", with a few exceptions such as defaults. Read in full.
- Ferraro, P. J., & Tracy, J. D. (2022). A reassessment of the potential for loss-framed incentive contracts to increase productivity: A meta-analysis and a real-effort experiment. *Experimental Economics*, 25(5), 1441–1466. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-022-09754-x Supports: carrying no size from a lab study. The summary effect of loss framing was 0.16 SD: 0.33 SD in laboratory experiments and 0.02 SD in field experiments. The authors attribute the gap to underpowered laboratory designs and publication bias rather than to the lab's limited external validity. Abstract only.
- Vivalt, E. (2020). How much can we generalize from impact evaluations? *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 18(6), 3045–3089. https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvaa019 Supports: lowering the central value when a government delivers. Across a data set of impact evaluation results, government-implemented programs had smaller effects than academic- or NGO-implemented ones, even controlling for sample size. Abstract only.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: the low end at one sixth of the high end when no unselected estimate exists (1.4 ÷ 8.7 = 0.16, from DellaVigna and Linos); a meta-analysis of the same intervention outranking single trials above it for the high end; and the 3-point check on the central value of a message, letter, or SMS, read from BIT's 2 to 3 points.

The direction of the correction held wherever it was checked. Its size depends on how selectively a field publishes and on who delivers the program, and both differ by setting: development trials may be less inflated than psychology studies, and a program can lose its effect when a government takes it over.

## Replication

Held: the direction of the gap, across published against unselected nudge trials, laboratory against field studies of loss framing, uncorrected against corrected nudge averages, and NGO against government delivery in Kenya and across development impact evaluations.

Contested: the size of the correction. For the same nudge data, corrected averages run from −0.01 to 0.31 SD depending on the method and the assumed severity of bias (Maier et al.; Szaszi et al.). Mertens et al. replied to these letters; the reply was not read. Ferraro and Tracy attribute the laboratory-to-field gap to underpowered laboratory designs and publication bias, not to the laboratory itself. Banerjee et al. show that a program that failed in a first government scale-up can succeed in a redesigned one.

Single-setting: the one-sixth ratio comes from two US nudge units only.

## What to check locally

1. Whether the program or its partners have run trials of the same kind before, and what all of them found, including the ones that changed nothing. That record is the unselected estimate the low end should come from.
2. The baseline rate or standard deviation, from the program's own data.
3. Take-up: the share of people offered the intervention who will receive it, such as working phone numbers or attendance.
4. Who will deliver the intervention at scale, and whether they have the staff and time the source's implementer had.
