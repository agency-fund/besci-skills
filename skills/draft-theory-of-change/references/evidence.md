# Evidence: Write the causal chain from activities to outcome

## Sources

- Mayne, J. (2015). Useful theory of change models. *Canadian Journal of Program Evaluation*, 30(2), 119–142. https://doi.org/10.3138/cjpe.230 Supports: the stages of the chain and the assumption on every link. Mayne's generic model runs from activities and outputs through reach and reaction, capacity changes, behavior changes, and direct benefits to changes in well-being. He writes that an impact pathway or logic model "is not a theory of change" until assumptions are added to the causal links, and defines those assumptions as the "salient events and conditions" that have to occur for each link to work. He also proposes checking, before a program starts, how much evidence supports each link. Read in full.
- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., Craig, P., Baird, J., Blazeby, J. M., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: writing the theory first and using it to find what is uncertain. The framework makes "programme theory" one of six core elements: it "describes how an intervention is expected to lead to its effects and under what conditions", should be developed at the start with the people affected, and is used "to identify key uncertainties and research questions". It asks for the theory in words as well as in a diagram. Read in full.
- Jang, C., Koki, E., Nyaga, R., Okafor, A., Singh, J., Vang, A., & Wendel, S. (2024). *The Busara toolkit: leveraging behavioral science for development*. Busara Groundwork No. 10. https://doi.org/10.62372/WQSB6195 Supports: stating the causal pathways and revising them. Section 2.2.3, "Develop a theory of change", asks teams to explain "why and how the program should affect the outcome variables of interest", define the causal pathways, and update the theory through the project. Section read in full.
- Fernandez, M. E., Ruiter, R. A. C., Markham, C. M., & Kok, G. (2019). Intervention Mapping: Theory- and evidence-based health promotion program planning: Perspective and examples. *Frontiers in Public Health*, 7, 209. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2019.00209 Supports: naming the behavior of those who deliver as well as those who benefit. Step 2 of Intervention Mapping, the "logic model of change", sets out what the at-risk group needs to do and what "those responsible for making changes in the environment" need to do. Read in full.

These rules are this skill's own and come from no source: the four strength labels, filling the evidence cell only from what the user supplied, and naming the weakest link nearest the outcome.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026. The skill is a drafting procedure, so this table records where its sources come from and what reviews say about how theories of change are written in practice.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Mayne (2015)](https://doi.org/10.3138/cjpe.230) | Methods paper; full text | Written in Canada; its running example is a hypothetical child nutrition program | No test of the method is reported |
| [Skivington et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061) | Research guidance; full text | United Kingdom | No test of the method is reported |
| [Jang et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.62372/WQSB6195) | Practitioner toolkit; section read | Written by a research group based in Kenya for development projects | No test of the method is reported |
| [Vadrevu, Jain & Parsekar (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-024-00615-2) | Review of 92 impact evaluations of immunization programs; abstract only | Low- and middle-income countries | 19% used a theory of change, 25% gave no account of how the intervention was expected to work, and only 19 of 92 stated any assumptions |
| [Breuer, Lee, De Silva & Lund (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13012-016-0422-6) | Systematic review of 62 papers on public health interventions; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Theories of change are widely used, but descriptions of how they were developed and used lacked detail |

No study was found that compares programs designed with a written theory of change against programs designed without one. The procedure does not depend on the population. The evidence in each cell does, and so does who has to act for a link to work: Mayne's own example of a failed link is a nutrition program aimed at mothers who do not decide what the children eat.

## Replication

Not applicable. No source tests whether two teams would draw the same chain for the same program, or whether a chain written this way leads to a better program.

## What to check locally

1. Whether each piece of evidence was collected in this program's population. If not, the link is "tested elsewhere" at most.
2. Whether the person each activity reaches is the person who decides. A link fails when the program trains or messages someone who does not control the behavior.
3. Whether the activities listed are the ones running, not the ones planned.
4. Whether the behaviors marked "inferred" are the ones the team relies on.
