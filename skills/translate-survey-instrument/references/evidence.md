# Evidence: Translate a survey into another language

## Sources

- Kopper, S., & Parry, K. (2023). *Survey design*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/survey-design Supports: the team and the register. Its translation section sets out four steps: "have a fluent speaker translate the survey"; have it "proofread by a second, independent fluent translator"; "have an independent translator to back-translate the text", ideally "someone who is unfamiliar with the survey", with "a further translator" to adjudicate differences; and "Determine how to reconcile differences". It adds: "Translate informed consent statements"; "Use simple, everyday language rather than formal language"; ask a fluent speaker when a technical term is better left in the source language; "fluency in both languages is not a sufficient condition of a good translation"; and, where field staff translate, "it is useful for several staff to work on the translation together to reach a consensus". It also says mistakes are often caught in enumerator training and that changes must be documented. Read in full. This skill departs from the guide in one respect: the guide makes back-translation a standard step, and the skill makes it a recorded decision, because of the experiments below.
- European Social Survey (2022). *ESS Round 11 Translation Guidelines*. ESS ERIC Headquarters. https://www.europeansocialsurvey.org/sites/default/files/2024-08/ESS_R11_Translation_Guidelines.pdf Supports: the order of work and the roles. "TRAPD is an acronym for Translation, Review, Adjudication, Pre-testing and Documentation", which the guidelines credit to Harkness, J. A. (2003), Questionnaire translation, in J. A. Harkness, F. van de Vijver, & P. Ph. Mohler (Eds.), *Cross-Cultural Survey Methods* (pp. 35–56), Wiley. The survey "calls for at least two translators per questionnaire", a reviewer, and an adjudicator, and describes this team approach as "qualitatively better than others (such as the much-cited 'back translation' approach)". On register: "survey translations often call for words people say rather than words people write". Section 3 read; the Harkness chapter itself was not.
- Behr, D., & Braun, M. (2023). How does back translation fare against team translation? An experimental case study in the language combination English–German. *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology*, 11(2), 285–315. https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smac005 Supports: back-translation is never the only check. Abstract only.
- Epstein, J., Osborne, R. H., Elsworth, G. R., Beaton, D. E., & Guillemin, F. (2015). Cross-cultural adaptation of the Health Education Impact Questionnaire: experimental study showed expert committee, not back-translation, added value. *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 68(4), 360–369. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2013.07.013 Supports: review by a group. Abstract only.
- Behr, D. (2017). Assessing the use of back translation: the shortcomings of back translation as a quality testing method. *International Journal of Social Research Methodology*, 20(6), 573–584. https://doi.org/10.1080/13645579.2016.1252188 Supports: treating a back-translation mismatch as a question and a clean result as no proof. Abstract only.
- Epstein, J., Santo, R. M., & Guillemin, F. (2015). A review of guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of questionnaires could not bring out a consensus. *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 68(4), 435–441. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2014.11.021 Supports: the replication note. Abstract only.
- International Test Commission (2018). ITC guidelines for translating and adapting tests (second edition). *International Journal of Testing*, 18(2), 101–134. https://doi.org/10.1080/15305058.2017.1398166 Supports: documenting the work. Its 18 guidelines fall into six groups, of which documentation is one. Abstract only.
- Scott, K., Ummer, O., & LeFevre, A. E. (2021). The devil is in the detail: reflections on the value and application of cognitive interviewing to strengthen quantitative surveys in global health. *Health Policy and Planning*, 36(6), 982–995. https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czab048 Supports: testing with respondents after translating. Back-translation "does not examine cultural appropriateness or the extent to which questions achieve cognitive match between researchers and respondents". Read in full.
- Bjärkefur, K., Cardoso de Andrade, L., Daniels, B., & Jones, M. R. (2021). *Development Research in Practice: The DIME Analytics Data Handbook*. World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1694-9 Supports: testing after translating. Its content-focused pilot is where a team refines "the phrasing and translation of specific questions". Chapter 4 read.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: sorting items into close translation, adaptation, or concept in doubt before anyone translates; recording in the log whether later rounds should leave a departure alone; and labeling the AI's own target-language wording as a draft.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Behr & Braun (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smac005) | Experiment: one first translation of international survey questions finished by a team or by back-translation, then a split-sample web survey; abstract only | English to German; country of the survey not stated in the abstract | "None of the twenty-nine issues in the initial translation were present in the final team translation version", and twenty-two remained after back-translation. In the survey, 5 of 15 items gave different answers, and only one difference could be traced to a remaining error |
| [Epstein, Osborne, Elsworth, Beaton & Guillemin (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2013.07.013) | Experiment: four translations made with and without a committee and back-translation, given at random to about 4,000 patients; abstract only | English to French; first author based in France | Fifteen bilingual assessors ranked the committee translations best. All four versions had good structural properties. The authors conclude that "back-translation has moderate impact, whereas expert committee helps to ensure accurate content" |
| [Behr (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1080/13645579.2016.1252188) | Re-analysis of the back-translation records of a European survey; abstract only | 2012 European Quality of Life Survey | Different people comparing the same back-translation flagged different flaws. Back-translation "causes quite a number of false alarms, and even more importantly, many problems remain hidden" |
| [Epstein, Santo & Guillemin (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2014.11.021) | Review of 31 guidelines; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | No consensus on method. "Evidence for the best methods is lacking, although clues indicate that back translation may not be mandatory" |
| [Scott et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czz141) | Cognitive interviews with 21 rural women on 88 questions adapted from instruments validated in other settings; abstract only | India | The interviews "revealed widespread mismatch between respondent interpretation and question intent", and many key terms "did not translate well" |

The scan found no study in a low- or middle-income country that compares translation methods, and no study of translation into languages with few trained translators. It searched for experiments on back-translation against team or committee translation, for the same in Africa, Asia, and low- and middle-income countries, for evaluations of team translation in African and South Asian languages, and for studies of interviewers translating during the interview.

What depends on local conditions is who can do the work and what the language offers: whether trained translators exist for the language, how far the spoken variety is from the written standard, and whether the idea behind an item exists in the same form.

## Replication

Contested. Two experiments and one re-analysis, all between European languages, find that team review catches more than back-translation, and one of them finds that most of the errors back-translation left in did not change survey answers. A review of 31 guidelines found that most include committees and back-translation, with no agreement on which steps are needed and little evidence either way. J-PAL's guide keeps back-translation as a standard step.

## What to check locally

1. Which variety respondents speak, and how far it is from the written standard the translators learned at school.
2. What respondents call the things the survey asks about (a medicine, a crop input, a clinic).
3. Whether each idea exists in the same form: the reviewers' failure to agree on words for an item is the signal.
4. Whether a tested translation of a published scale already exists in this language.
5. Whether anyone requires back-translation, and whether a reviewer who reads both languages is available.
