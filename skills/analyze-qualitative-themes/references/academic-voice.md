# Writing in a natural academic voice

The rules for research prose that `write-in-academic-voice` applies and that report-writing skills in this library follow. They are Zezhen Wu's revision rules, adapted for the library.

Order of priority: correctness, then precision, then clarity, then a natural voice, then the style rules below. Never trade precision for a sentence that sounds better.

## The aim

Write like an experienced researcher explaining an argument to others in the field: clear, specific, and serious, without sounding inflated, mechanical, or polished to a shine. Naturalness comes from varied syntax, qualification that matches the evidence, and attention to the argument. Do not add errors or awkwardness to seem human.

## Do not use

- Em dashes or en dashes, unless the journal or a quoted source requires them.
- A semicolon between two independent clauses when a full stop or a conjunction works. Leave punctuation inside quotations, citations, titles, and technical notation alone.
- Ellipses, except inside quotations.
- Clichés and promotional language.
- Staged sequences ("first, second, finally") unless the argument has numbered steps.
- Self-narration: "let us explore", "we now turn to", "it is worth noting", "this section will discuss".
- Clusters of rhetorical questions.
- An opening sentence that restates the heading.
- Pivots such as "X; rather, Y", "X, rather than Y", and "not X, but Y". State the claim directly. Keep a comparison only when both of its terms matter to the argument, or when it corrects a real misconception.
- "Not only ... but also" where a direct sentence works.
- Introductory or concluding sentences that make no claim.
- Generalizations with no support, and "research shows" or "studies suggest" without naming the research.
- Comments on whether the writing sounds human or machine-made.

## Words to avoid

Avoid these when a plainer or more exact word works: tapestry, landscape (as a figure of speech), interplay, intricacies, multifaceted, paradigm, delve, foster, garner, underscore, showcase, pivotal, crucial, vibrant, intricate, profound, compelling, poignant, arguably, notably, importantly, ultimately, fundamentally, inherently, undeniably.

Also avoid: shed light on, harness, illuminate, unveil, elucidate, revolutionize, groundbreaking, cutting-edge, transformative, game-changing, navigating the complexities of, in today's rapidly changing world, remains to be seen, in summary, in conclusion.

A technical term with a specific meaning in the field stays, even if it is on a list above.

Prefer ordinary verbs: use (not utilize); help (not facilitate, when they mean the same); show, find, report, indicate, or suggest (not highlight or underscore); improve (not enhance, unless something more specific is meant); field, literature, setting, or context (not landscape); important or central (not crucial or pivotal).

## Tone

- Write for researchers who know the broad field. Explain a specialized concept when the reader may not know it.
- Prefer a precise claim to broad commentary. Cut adjectives and adverbs that carry nothing.
- Use the active voice where it is natural. The passive is right when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or conventional in a method description.
- Use "we" or "I" for the authors' argument, method, and interpretation. Avoid "you" unless the genre calls for it. Avoid contractions in formal manuscripts.
- Qualify each claim to the strength of its evidence, and keep evidence, interpretation, speculation, and recommendation apart. Do not remove a hedge to sound decisive.

## Sentences and paragraphs

- Vary sentence length as the content requires. Most sentences carry one main claim. A longer sentence is right when it has to express how ideas relate.
- Avoid a run of sentences with the same length or the same grammatical opening.
- Keep parallel structure where it clarifies. Do not manufacture symmetry.
- A paragraph needs a purpose, not a fixed pattern. It may open with an observation, continue the previous argument, add a boundary condition, give an example before its implication, or weigh competing explanations.
- Do not end every paragraph by restating its point. Some paragraphs end on evidence.
- Let paragraph length follow the difficulty of the idea.

## Argument and evidence

- Say what the evidence establishes and what remains uncertain. Make no claim stronger than its source supports.
- Keep correlation, prediction, mechanism, and causation distinct.
- State an assumption when the argument depends on it.
- Include counterevidence, limitations, and alternative explanations when they affect the conclusion. Do not build a balanced "pros and cons" structure the literature does not support.
- Do not force ideas into three or four parts unless the categories come from the theory, the data, or the literature.
- Name authors, studies, data sets, and findings. Keep numbers that matter: dates, sample sizes, estimates, intervals.
- Never invent a citation, a finding, a mechanism, or an anecdote. When evidence is mixed, describe the disagreement.
- Separate what a source reports from what you make of it.

## Terms and transitions

- Use one term for one construct throughout. Do not rotate synonyms to avoid repetition: if the construct is "ecological validity", it stays "ecological validity". Vary the verbs and the sentence structure around it.
- Use a transition only when it states a logical relation: however, but, because, therefore, in contrast, for example, by comparison, accordingly.
- Do not open paragraphs by default with "Building on this", "Taken together", "Against this backdrop", "Importantly", "More broadly", or "At the same time".

## Patterns that make prose read as assembled

Watch for: every paragraph opening with a broad claim and closing with a summary; the same claim, evidence, implication order in every paragraph; repeated lists of three; many "X suggests that Y" sentences; repeated "while X, Y" contrasts; the same structure in every section; signposting that says what is coming; sentences that package an idea as a slogan; long noun phrases where a verb would be clearer.

Examples of the change to make:

- Before: "Importantly, these findings underscore the crucial role of social context in shaping the complex interplay between individual behavior and intervention outcomes." After: "These findings suggest that intervention effects depend partly on the social context in which participants respond."
- Before: "Reminders are not merely prompts; they represent a paradigm shift in how programs can support follow-through." After: "Reminders give programs one more way to support follow-through, at the moment a person would otherwise forget."
- Before: "While these approaches offer significant promise, they also present important challenges that must be carefully considered." After: "Several problems remain, particularly around validity, who is represented, and generalization."

## When revising someone's text

1. Keep the author's meaning unless asked to change the argument.
2. Keep every citation attached to the claim it supported.
3. Remove repeated claims before shortening an explanation that is needed.
4. Prefer concrete nouns and verbs to abstract nominalizations.
5. Break up a sentence only when that helps the reader.
6. Keep the field's terms, the author's qualifications, and the author's uncertainty.
7. Leave an accurate definition and its citation alone.
8. Check whether neighboring paragraphs make the same point in different words.
9. Match the author's existing voice. Do not replace it with a generic one, and do not polish every sentence to the same finish.

## Last pass

Before handing prose back, check for: claims the evidence does not support; citations that were added, dropped, or moved; inflated vocabulary; repeated openers; paragraphs that restate their first sentence at the end; needless summaries; synonyms standing in for a core term; overuse of "important", "suggest", "highlight", "underscore", and "demonstrate"; sentences all short or all long; polish that hides the claim; hedges that were lost; and imperfections added on purpose.

The finished prose should read as if a researcher worked the argument out, revised it for clarity, and kept the terms, the uncertainty, and the uneven difficulty that serious reasoning has.
