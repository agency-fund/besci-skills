# Evidence: Run a thematic analysis of interviews or open-ended responses

## Sources

- Braun, V., & Clarke, V. (2006). Using thematic analysis in psychology. *Qualitative Research in Psychology*, 3(2), 77–101. https://doi.org/10.1191/1478088706qp063oa Supports: the six phases (familiarizing yourself with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, producing the report); the terms data corpus, data set, data item, and data extract; the choices to make before analysis (what counts as a theme and how prevalence is judged, a description of the whole data set or one aspect, inductive or theoretical, semantic or latent, essentialist, contextualist, or constructionist); the two levels of review in phase 4; the thematic map; the five pitfalls; and the fifteen-point checklist. The paper also says that an account of themes "emerging" denies the analyst's active role, and that one way to show that role is to write in the first person. Full text, from the authors' manuscript.
- Clarke, V., & Braun, V. (2017). Thematic analysis. *The Journal of Positive Psychology*, 12(3), 297–298. https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2016.1262613 Supports: codes as "the smallest units of analysis that capture interesting features of the data"; themes as patterns of meaning "underpinned by a central organizing concept"; thematic analysis as a method, not a methodology; a research question that "is not fixed and can evolve throughout coding and theme development"; and the split between versions that foreground coding reliability and the authors' version, which stresses "an organic approach to coding and theme development and the active role of the researcher". It is a two-page commentary and contains no procedure. Full text.
- Jowsey, T., Braun, V., Clarke, V., Lupton, D., & Fine, M. (2026). We reject the use of generative artificial intelligence for reflexive qualitative research. *Qualitative Inquiry*, 32(10), 931–935. https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004251401851 Supports: the statement the skill makes to every user and in every report. The abstract says that 419 qualitative researchers from 32 countries hold that "analytic approaches such as reflexive thematic analysis are human research practices requiring a subjective, positioned, and reflexive researcher and therefore the use of GenAI in such approaches is not methodologically congruent", and that they also reject it on grounds of social and environmental justice. First published online in December 2025, and cited as 2025 in the replies to it; the issue is dated 2026. Abstract only.
- Greenhalgh, T. (2026). Reflexive qualitative research and generative AI: A call to go beyond the binary. *Qualitative Inquiry*, 32(10), 949–950. https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004261429383 Supports: the two ways of running the skill. A published reply that accepts the concerns about meaning-making, reflexivity, and bias, says they "do not justify categorical rejection of all AI use", and proposes "distinguishing AI-led analysis from human-led practices". Abstract only.
- Braun, V., & Clarke, V. (2021). One size fits all? What counts as quality practice in (reflexive) thematic analysis? *Qualitative Research in Psychology*, 18(3), 328–352. https://doi.org/10.1080/14780887.2020.1769238 Supports: not using agreement between coders as the test of quality. The abstract notes that editors and reviewers "promote 'coding reliability measures' as universal requirements of quality TA", and treats this as a problem. Abstract only.
- Gierbolini-Rivera, R. D., Franco Silva, M., Augusto de Paula da Silva, A., Brownson, R. C., Parra, D. C., Kepper, M. M., & Eyler, A. A. (2026). Generative artificial intelligence for qualitative methods in health research: Rapid review. *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, 28, e98551. https://doi.org/10.2196/98551 Supports: the extract check and the default to surface meaning in a run on its own. Structured abstract only.
- De Paoli, S. (2024). Performing an inductive thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews with a large language model: An exploration and provocation on the limits of the approach. *Social Science Computer Review*, 42(4), 997–1019. https://doi.org/10.1177/08944393231220483 Supports: checking code labels after they are merged, and keeping phase 4 under challenge. Read as the arXiv preprint, version 4.
- Ashwin, J., Chhabra, A., & Rao, V. (2026). Using large language models for qualitative analysis can introduce serious bias. *Sociological Methods & Research*, 55(3), 795–839. https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241251338246 Supports: comparing how each group of participants was coded. First published online in May 2025; the issue is dated 2026. Abstract only.
- Liu, A., Esbenshade, L., Xiao, M., Tian, V., Zhang, Z., He, K., & Sun, M. (2026). *Agreement is not quality: Blind expert verification of human and LLM qualitative coding when human consensus is not ground truth*. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28890 Supports: not reading agreement, between agents or with a person, as a measure of quality. A preprint, not peer reviewed. Abstract only.

The authors now call their approach reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019, https://doi.org/10.1080/2159676X.2019.1628806, abstract only) and have renamed phases 2 to 6: Coding; Generating initial themes; Developing and reviewing themes; Refining, defining and naming themes; Writing up (Braun & Clarke, *Doing reflexive TA*, https://www.thematicanalysis.net/doing-reflexive-ta/, read November 2026). This skill keeps the 2006 phases and wording.

Thematic analysis is also a named step in three of the library's canonical sources: the Busara toolkit (section 2.2.1, which cites Braun and Clarke, 2006), J-PAL's *Implementing qualitative methods in the field* ("Analyzing qualitative data"), and UNICEF's SBC guidance ("Diagnosing the Situation", step 2). The J-PAL resource recommends checking agreement between coders, which this skill does not do.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026. The skill is a procedure, so this table records what is known about AI systems carrying it out.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Gierbolini-Rivera et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.2196/98551) | Rapid review of 42 studies in health research, 2022 to 2025; abstract only | Not given in the abstract | Agreement with human analysts "often exceeding 80% for descriptive themes" and "dropping to approximately 30% for culturally nuanced themes"; "superficial interpretations", misapplied frameworks, and "fabricated quotes"; human oversight "consistently identified as essential" |
| [De Paoli (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/08944393231220483) | Single study; preprint read in full | Interviews from a European Union project and a United States university | An early model taken through phases 1 to 5 recovered "most of the main themes from previous research"; it sometimes invented code names when merging codes, and one theme may have been invented; phase 4 leaned most on human judgment |
| [Ashwin et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241251338246) | Single study; abstract only | Bangladesh: Rohingya refugees and their Bengali hosts | Coding errors were "not random with respect to the characteristics of the interview subjects", which can mislead comparisons between groups. It tested coding to a fixed scheme, not building themes |
| [Liu et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28890) | Single study; preprint, abstract only | Not given in the abstract | Models agreed with human coders less than humans agreed with each other, yet a blind expert preferred human and model coding at about the same rate |

The procedure does not depend on the population. The reading does. Culturally specific, emotional, and underlying meaning is where AI readings were weakest, and that is the part a person who knows the participants and the setting has to supply. Only one study found uses data from outside wealthy Western countries.

## Replication

Not applicable to the method: Braun and Clarke do not expect two analysts to arrive at the same themes, and they do not treat agreement as the test of a good analysis. For AI running the phases, the evidence is new and contested. The method's authors reject the use (Jowsey et al., 2026), and published replies disagree with a blanket rejection (Greenhalgh, 2026).

## What to check locally

1. Whether participants agreed to their words being processed by an AI service, and whether the data can be de-identified first.
2. Whether the data are word-for-word transcripts, an interviewer's notes, or translations, and who can check a theme against the original language.
3. Whether someone who collected the data or knows the setting has read two or three items against the themes, above all when the run was on its own.
4. Whether items from each group of participants (by sex, language, site, or status) were coded as fully as the rest.
5. Whether any item discloses harm, and whether the safeguarding response has happened.
