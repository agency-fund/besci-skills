# The choices to settle before coding

The decisions Braun and Clarke (2006) ask an analyst to make and report, each as a question a program designer can answer, with what the skill assumes when nobody answers.

Thematic analysis is a method with no theory built in, so two analysts can run it on the same interviews and rightly produce different things. Braun and Clarke's point is that the choices below get made either way. Left unstated, an analysis usually ends up describing what people said and treating it as a plain report of their experience, without anyone having decided that. Write the choices in `working/plan.md` and report them in the Method section.

## Terms

- **Data corpus:** everything collected for the project.
- **Data set:** the part of the corpus this analysis uses. It can be whole items (the twelve farmer interviews, not the staff ones) or every passage on one topic across all items.
- **Data item:** one interview, one focus group, one survey response.
- **Data extract:** a coded piece of a data item.
- **Code:** a short label for one feature of the data that bears on the question. The smallest unit of the analysis.
- **Theme:** a pattern of meaning across the data set, held together by one central idea, that says something about the question. A theme is built from codes. A topic ("training") is not yet a theme. A claim about it ("training is remembered as a test, not as help") can be.

## 1. The question

Ask: "What do you want to understand from this material?"

A question can be broad ("how do health workers experience the app?") or narrow ("why do workers enter visits they did not make?"). Clarke and Braun (2017) note that the question need not be fixed: in data-driven work it can sharpen as coding goes on. Keep three kinds of question apart: the research question, the questions put to participants, and the questions the analyst asks of the data. Themes that mirror the interview guide mean no analysis has been done.

If nobody gives a question: draft a broad one from what the material is about, say it is a draft, and revisit it after coding.

## 2. The whole data set, or one aspect in depth

Ask: "Do you want a picture of everything important in here, or a close look at one thing?"

- **Whole data set:** the themes together reflect the important content of all of it. Some depth is lost. Suits a topic nobody has studied or participants whose views are unknown.
- **One aspect:** a detailed account of one theme or one question. The rest of the data is left out on purpose, and the report says so.

Default: the whole data set.

## 3. Data-driven or theory-driven

Ask: "Should the themes come from what people said, or are you checking the data against a framework you already have?"

- **Data-driven (inductive):** code without a prior frame. The themes may have little to do with the questions that were asked. Reading the literature can wait until after coding.
- **Theory-driven (deductive, or "theoretical"):** code for what a framework or a specific question points to. Read the framework first. This gives less of an overall description and more detail on one part.

No analyst is free of assumptions, so "data-driven" means no coding frame, not no viewpoint. If the team has named hypotheses about why a behavior is not happening and wants a verdict on each, that is a different task with a different output: `synthesize-interview-findings`.

Default: data-driven.

## 4. Surface meaning or underlying meaning

Ask: "Do you want what people said, organized and interpreted, or the assumptions underneath what they said?"

- **Surface (semantic):** themes are found in what was explicitly said. The analysis still interprets: it moves from describing the pattern to saying what it means and implies.
- **Underlying (latent):** themes name the ideas, assumptions, and beliefs that shape what was said. Building the themes is already interpretation.

An analysis works mainly at one level. Default: surface. An underlying-meaning analysis leans on knowing the participants' world. In the autonomous mode, say that the reading comes from a model with no membership in that world, and prefer the mode with the researcher.

## 5. How to treat what people say

Ask: "Are you taking people's words as a report of their experience, or looking at how their setting shapes what can be said?"

- **As a report of experience (essentialist or realist):** language reflects meaning and experience, so the analysis can speak of motivations and experiences directly.
- **As shaped by social context (constructionist):** meaning is produced socially, so the analysis looks at the conditions that make these accounts possible, not at individual motives.
- **Between the two (contextualist, for example critical realism):** people make meaning of their experience, and the wider setting bears on that meaning.

This choice limits the claims the report may make. An experience-based analysis does not make claims about social construction. A constructionist one does not treat talk as a window onto someone's world. Default: a report of experience, read with attention to the setting.

## 6. What counts as a theme, and how prevalence is reported

There is no share of the data set above which something becomes a theme. A theme can run through most items or matter greatly in a few. What makes it a theme is that it captures something important about the question.

Decide once, and keep to it:

- **The unit for prevalence:** data items (in how many interviews does it appear), speakers, or single occurrences. Default: data items.
- **The wording:** if the report says "most" or "a few", say what those words stand for, and give the count ("in 9 of the 12 interviews"). A count describes the people interviewed, not a population. Never turn it into a percentage of farmers, mothers, or users.

## 7. Reading the literature

Data-driven work is better served by leaving the literature until the themes exist. Theory-driven work needs the framework first. The skill cites only the method papers and sources the user supplies, each checked before it is cited.

## Which choices go together

Whole data set, data-driven, surface meaning, and experience-based tend to go together. One aspect, theory-driven, underlying meaning, and constructionist tend to go together. Other combinations are legitimate. What the paper requires is that the choices are stated, that they fit each other, and that the report's claims stay inside them.
