# Evidence: Plan how a tested program reaches routine delivery

## Sources

- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2010). *Nine steps for developing a scaling-up strategy*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241500319 Supports: the whole procedure. The guide's framework has five elements, the innovation, the user organization, the environment, the resource team, and the scaling-up strategy at the center, and five strategic choice areas: the type of scaling up, dissemination and advocacy, the organizational process, costs and resource mobilization, and monitoring and evaluation. Its nine steps are: increasing the scalability of the innovation; the capacity of the user organization; assessing the environment; the capacity of the resource team; strategic choices for vertical scaling up (institutionalization); strategic choices for horizontal scaling up (expansion or replication); the role of diversification; spontaneous scaling up; and finalizing the strategy. Step 1 asks the team to list "all components", hardware and software, and to answer "Is it still appropriate to proceed with plans for scaling up?". Table 5.1 lists the changes needed to institutionalize an innovation, each marked yes, no, or unknown: policy, political commitment, legal change, regulations, norms and guidelines, financing and budgets, logistics, management information systems, supervision, staff evaluation and performance incentives, training curricula and approaches, health workforce changes, and information, education and communication materials. The guide says that "ownership of the scaling-up process belongs to the user organization and not to the resource team"; that rapid institutionalization is desirable while rapid expansion "can often result in a loss of essential components"; that diversification should wait until the original innovation is soundly implemented; and that step 9 leaves out recommendations "that cannot be acted upon" and ends in operational plans that "indicate who has responsibility for what part within what period of time".
- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2011). *Beginning with the end in mind*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241502320 Supports: listing the pilot's hidden inputs. The guide's premise is that "pilot projects tend to be implemented with a level of input and support that subsequently cannot be sustained when innovations are taken to scale".
- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: the rule that every action has an owner. The guide's activity for a roll-out is to plan for scale-up "including obtaining sign-off, planning logistics, and allocating responsibility", with the SCALE questions (Sponsorship, Cost/benefit, Accountability, Logistics, Evidence) as the worksheet.
- Fernandez, M. E., Ruiter, R. A. C., Markham, C. M., & Kok, G. (2019). Intervention Mapping: Theory- and evidence-based health promotion program planning: Perspective and examples. *Frontiers in Public Health*, 7, 209. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2019.00209 Supports: separating who decides from who delivers. Step 5 of Intervention Mapping plans adoption, implementation, and maintenance, "as well as who has to do what at each of these stages and why", and can be used "to develop plans for scale up and spread of existing evidence-based interventions". The overview notes that people who do not deliver a program, such as school principals, may decide whether it is adopted.

The rule that each wave of expansion waits on a named open test, and the definitions of the rows in the output template, are this skill's own.

The steps do not depend on the country. Every answer does: who owns the program, when the budget opens, what the law allows, and who can supervise at scale.

## Replication

Not applicable. None of the sources tests whether programs scaled with a written strategy of this kind last longer or reach more people than programs scaled without one.

## What to check locally

1. Whether one organization will own the program, or several, each needing a lead and a defined role.
2. When the budget cycle opens and who decides what enters it.
3. Whether any part of the program needs a change in law or regulation, such as who may dispense or counsel.
4. Whether supervisors at scale can give the supervision the pilot had.
5. Whether others are already copying the program, and what they leave out.
