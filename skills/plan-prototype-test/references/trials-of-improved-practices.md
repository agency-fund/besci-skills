# Home trial: Trials of Improved Practices

How to fill the prototype test plan when the draft is a practice that people try at home over several days: a way of feeding a child, a handwashing step, a way of storing tablets.

## Whose method it is

Trials of Improved Practices (TIPs), also called household trials, comes from child-feeding programs. Its manual was written by two authors at the Manoff Group and one at the Academy for Educational Development: Dickin, K., Griffiths, M., & Piwoz, E. (1997). *Designing by Dialogue: A Program Planners' Guide to Consultative Research for Improving Young Child Feeding*. Academy for Educational Development. https://web.archive.org/web/20241203073137/https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PNACE296.pdf

The manual describes the method this way (chapters 1, 4, and 6):

- A field worker visits the home, learns what the family does now, and presents several practices that fit the problem found. The caregiver is "asked to select among them". The two agree on "one, two, or, at most, three specific changes" to try, and the caregiver should feel that the decision was hers.
- The field worker checks understanding by asking the caregiver "to repeat in her own words what new practice she is going to try and how she will do it".
- The trial lasts "usually about one week". The follow-up visit comes five or six days after the practices are agreed.
- At follow-up the field worker asks how far the caregiver followed the practice and why, whether it was hard or easy, what other people thought, whether anyone benefited or was harmed, whether she changed the practice and why, and whether she will continue.
- Two protocols exist. Three visits: an initial visit to assess, a counseling visit to agree the practices, and a follow-up. Two visits, with the first two combined, when the team already knows current practice well and the field workers can assess on the spot.
- The sample is purposive: 10 to 15 children per site, or two to three in each age group per site.
- The tally records, for each practice, what was offered, what was agreed, and the outcome: "was it kept, modified, or not followed, and why".
- "Negative reactions and unsuccessful adoption are as important as positive reactions and successful adoption."

The manual separates this from testing materials: a home trial tests whether people can carry out the practice, and messages are pretested later.

## When to choose it

Choose a home trial when the learning question is whether people will keep doing a practice in their own homes, and what they change about it. Choose another method when the draft is something a person reads or handles once.

## How it fills the plan

**Draft under test.** Replace the single draft with a practice menu:

| # | Practice (who, what, when, how often) | Barrier it addresses | What the household needs | Smaller version |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | <...> | <...> | <materials, time, money> | <what a household can do if the full practice is too much> |

**Who and how many.** Households, grouped by what may change the result: who does the practice, who decides, distance, season. Use the manual's numbers as a starting point, and make sure several households try each practice.

**Session guide.** Two visits replace the single session.

Visit 1:
1. Ask what happened yesterday, or watch, to learn the current practice.
2. Name the gap in the family's own terms. Present only the practices that fit it.
3. The household chooses one, and at most three. Agree when, with what, and who.
4. Ask the person to say in their own words what they will try.
5. Ask what they expect to be hard, and whether anyone else must agree.

Set the trial period from how often the practice happens, so that each household has several occasions to do it: about a week for a daily practice, longer for a weekly one.

Visit 2:
1. Did they try it, and how many times?
2. What did they change, and why, in their words?
3. What did other people in the household say?
4. Did anyone benefit, or come to harm?
5. Will they continue, and what would make it easier?

**Recording sheet.** One row per household per practice:

| Household | Practice | Times tried | Kept / modified / not followed | Modification | Reason (their words) | Others' reaction | Will continue? | Safety concern? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Decision rule.** Fix it before visit 1, per practice:

- Keep: most households that tried the practice kept it, or modified it in a way that still does the job.
- Change and retest: the most common modification defeats the purpose. Redraft the practice around what households did.
- Drop: most households did not follow it, or the common reason was cost or safety.
- List in advance which modifications still do the job and which defeat it.

**What this test cannot show.** A home trial shows that families can do a practice at home for a week in this season. It does not show that the practice improves health, or that families will still be doing it in six months.

## What this skill adds to the manual

Four rules here are this skill's own and are not in the manual: every practice on the menu names the barrier it addresses; every practice has a smaller version, so that a household that cannot manage the full practice shows what it can manage; the decision rule is written before fieldwork; and the modifications that still do the job are listed in advance.

## What goes wrong in a home trial

- **The field worker assigns and the household complies.** If visit 1 ends with "please do this", visit 2 measures politeness.
- **"Tried once" read as "kept".** Record times tried and the intention to continue separately.
- **A supplied item hides the cost.** A practice that depends on a scoop or soap the program handed out is a different practice without it.
- **One gain offset by another change.** The manual warns that feeding more often can mean less food at each meal. Ask what else changed.
