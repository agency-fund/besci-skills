# Evidence: Test a draft with a few people before the pilot

## Sources

The task is a named step in these process guides:

- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: the plan's parts. "User testing" in the Solution stage is planned in advance around three things: the learning questions, which "should be linked to the barriers the intervention is meant to address"; who to test with, where the number "can range from as few as five - if the group of end users is very homogenous and likely to have the same views - to a larger group"; and how, "Will you show it to the end user, ask them to use it, or simply describe the idea?". Also supports having people use the draft: in the guide's Indonesian example, business owners were asked to fill in a savings calendar "instead of just asking them whether they do, to which they might answer 'yes' to please us". The guide says the process "does not show us whether the actual intervention will have an impact", and that a test may end in dropping the idea. Read in full.
- Gyani, A., Tindall, K., & Gallagher, R. (2022). *Explore Field Guide*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Explore-Field-Guide.pdf Supports: matching the version to the question. Low-fidelity prototypes (a sketch, a process map with role plays, a storyboard) show whether an idea is worth pursuing; high-fidelity prototypes are used "to iron out kinks", with participants asked to use the draft "as they would in the field" and to think aloud. Read in full.

Four rules are this skill's own and come from no source: the keep, change, or drop rule is written before the first session; the draft's author does not run the sessions; at least two rounds are planned; and results are reported as counts.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| [Faulkner (2003)](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03195514) | Single study, 60 users, resampled in groups; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract; the author worked at a United States university | Some random groups of five found 99% of the problems and others 55%. The procedure does not depend on the population. The session does: whether people will criticize a draft in front of the tester, whether saying one's thoughts aloud to a stranger is ordinary, and whether a task that goes wrong is felt as the person's own failure. The figures on how many people find how many problems come from software, and no study found tests them on paper materials, scripts, or practices.

## Replication

The claim that a few people surface most problems has held only in part: Virzi's 80% with four or five participants is an average, and Faulkner found groups of five that caught as little as 55%. The finding that people favor what they think the interviewer made rests on one study, in one city. The finding that thinking aloud draws less from Indian and Kenyan users rests on one paper with five people per group. No source tests whether a prototype test leads to a better intervention.

## What to check locally

1. In front of whom people will criticize a draft. Compare what participants say to a tester from their own community with what they say to an outsider, in the first sessions.
2. Whether thinking aloud works. Try it in the first two sessions; if people fall silent, have them finish the task and then walk back through it.
3. Which groups in the audience differ in ways that matter for this draft: schooling, language, phone ownership, who decides at home.
4. Whether the test setting resembles the place the draft will be used. A card read at the clinic is not a card read at home three weeks later.
5. For a home trial, whether the trial week is typical for food, water, cash, and workload.
