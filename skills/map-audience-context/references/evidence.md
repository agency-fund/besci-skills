# Evidence: Map your audience and their setting

## Sources

- Jang, C., Koki, E., Nyaga, R., Okafor, A., Singh, J., Vang, A., & Wendel, S. (2024). *The Busara toolkit: leveraging behavioral science for development*. Busara Groundwork No. 10. https://doi.org/10.62372/WQSB6195 Supports: the decision moment and the source labels. Section 2.1.1 defines context as "the interaction between people (demographics, norms, personal history) with a place (physical environment, available resources) at a specific moment (including the person's mindset and the local stimuli) concerning a particular activity". Its three steps are to identify the context (people, places, activities), to catalog what the team already knows as insiders and as outsiders, and to choose a field visit or reading that fills the gap. Section read in full.
- The Technical and Operational Performance Support (TOPS) Program (2017). *Designing for Behavior Change: A Practical Field Guide*. Washington, DC: The TOPS Program. https://web.archive.org/web/20180329030310/http://fsnnetwork.org/sites/default/files/designing_for_behavior_change_a_practical_field_guide.pdf Supports: narrowing the population and naming who else has a say. The guide lists "Seven Ways to Describe the Priority Group", among them demographic features, daily routine, and gender. Its Influencing Groups are the people who approve or disapprove of the behavior, "usually very close to the Priority Group, such as someone's mother-in-law, or spouse". Read in full, from an archived copy.
- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., Craig, P., Baird, J., Blazeby, J. M., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: the constraints section. Context is one of the framework's six core elements; its dimensions "include physical, spatial, organisational, social, cultural, political, or economic features", and an intervention "that is effective in some settings could be ineffective or even harmful elsewhere". Read in full.
- Henrich, J., Heine, S. J., & Norenzayan, A. (2010). The weirdest people in the world? *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 33(2–3), 61–83. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X0999152X Supports: the assumption check. The review compares populations across the behavioral sciences, on domains that include cooperation, reasoning styles, and self-concepts, and finds that people from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic societies are "frequent outliers". Abstract only.

These rules are this skill's own and come from no source: the three source labels, listing only channels that exist today, refusing to name a place the input did not mention, and the two-part assumption check. The infrastructure half of that check (bank accounts, phones, literacy) rests on practice, not on a study.

A search for reviews of how programs describe their audience before design found guides and single case studies, and no review that compares methods. The procedure does not depend on the population. Every claim in a brief does, which is why each one carries a source label.

## Replication

Not applicable. No source tests whether two teams would describe the same audience alike, or whether programs that write a brief like this one choose better behaviors.

## What to check locally

1. Who decides, asked of members of the group away from the people who might have a say.
2. Whether the decision moment in the brief is where the choice is made, checked with more than one visit or informant.
3. Whether each channel reaches the narrowed group, not only people like them elsewhere in the country.
4. Whether the group uses a bank, a phone, or written material for this purpose, as a fact and not from national figures.
