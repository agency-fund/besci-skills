# Evidence: Write scenarios that measure what others do and approve

## Sources

- Bicchieri, C., Lindemans, J. W., & Jiang, T. (2014). A structured approach to a diagnostic of collective practices. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 5, 1418. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01418 Supports: the design, the questions, and three of the rules in step 1. "Vignettes tell short stories about imaginary characters in specific scenarios." They get round a known problem: asked what he would do if most people around him gave up a practice, a respondent is likely to say that this will never happen, and "it may be easier to answer hypothetical questions about fictitious characters than questions about actual family and friends". The design: "We can also vary what others in this fictional father's community are doing or what they are approving and disapproving of." Its example gives a base story about a father deciding on a daughter's marriage, one sentence on what most girls in the village do, and one on what people in the village say a good father does. The questions that follow ask whether he will agree, why, what might drive him to agree or to refuse, and "Do you think Mr. Badji should agree to the marriage?" The paper calls vignettes "quasi-experiments": the expectations changed are the fictional person's, and the method relies on respondents imagining "what they would do if they were in the position of the protagonist". It asks about "some other individual in another community". Its cautions: a vignette should not be "so complex or alien that the respondent has trouble understanding or relating to it", and "it is worth making any critical details explicitly stated so that respondents do not imagine the scenarios in different lights". Full text.
- Bicchieri, C., & Penn Social Norms Training and Consulting Group (2016). *Why Do People Do What They Do? A Social Norms Manual for Viet Nam, Indonesia and the Philippines*. UNICEF Office of Research – Innocenti. https://repository.upenn.edu/pennsong/12 Supports: who the fictional person is, and the terms used in step 7. Vignettes are "short stories or depictions of behaviour involving fictional characters with enough relevant similarity to those you are questioning"; the designer should "alternate or vary small details of the vignette to try to figure out when, for example, normative expectations matter". It warns that "the characters must be relevantly similar to the people you are surveying", and advises: "Work with experts to make sure that your vignette is contextually appropriate and getting at the right information." Its glossary defines a descriptive norm as a pattern people follow on condition that they expect others to, a social norm as one that also depends on what they believe others think they should do, and a custom and a moral norm as patterns people follow unconditionally. The pages on vignettes and the glossary read.
- Institute for Reproductive Health, Georgetown University (2020). *Social Norms Exploration Tool*. https://www.irh.org/social-norms-exploration/ Supports: the rules for the base story. Its vignette exercise is a story read to a group, with open questions at each pause; it does not assign versions at random. Its advice on the story matches the sources above: "The characters are similar to people in program communities so participants can relate to them", "the story and characters are not real and cannot be linked to real people", and "the story is simple yet complete: participants do not need to make assumptions and fill in missing information". The vignette exercise in Phase 3 and the sample vignette in Annex 6 read.
- Bicchieri, C. (2017). *Norms in the Wild: How to Diagnose, Measure, and Change Social Norms*. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780190622046.001.0001 Supports: the reason to vary expectations at all. The book covers how to "measure expectations and preferences". Abstract only.
- Atzmüller, C., & Steiner, P. M. (2010). Experimental vignette studies in survey research. *Methodology*, 6(3), 128–138. https://doi.org/10.1027/1614-2241/a000014 Supports: the caution in step 6. When each respondent sees only some of the versions, choosing them at random "heavily rely on the assumption of no interaction effects", while choosing them by an experimental design keeps "a meaningful interpretation of main and important interaction effects". Abstract only.
- World Bank (2015). *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0342-0 Supports: the task as a named technique. Box 11.2: "Eliciting information through vignettes or hypothetical situations about fictional people allows respondents to think about a situation in a way that is more emotionally removed from their personal concerns but that tends to reveal social expectations."
- UNICEF (2022). *Social & Behaviour Change at UNICEF: Programme Guidance*. https://www.sbcguidance.org/ Supports: using vignettes to measure norms before and after a program. Its table of methods for measuring social norms lists, at baseline and at endline, "quantitative surveys, qualitative interviews, vignettes in FGDs" (focus group discussions).
- Jang, C., Koki, E., Nyaga, R., Okafor, A., Singh, J., Vang, A., & Wendel, S. (2024). *The Busara toolkit: leveraging behavioral science for development*. Busara Groundwork No. 10. https://doi.org/10.62372/WQSB6195 Supports: reading the story aloud. "Photovoice, vignettes, and story completion are particularly effective for populations with limited literacy and for sensitive topics."

The check that the cue was heard, the rule for reading the pattern of answers, and the rule to stop before wording a story about a harmful practice are this skill's own. The reading rule applies the manual's definitions; it is not a published decision rule. The 2014 paper's example adds one cue to each story; putting both cues in every story, in four combinations, is this skill's choice, so that each cue can be read with the other held fixed.

None found: a study in a low- or middle-income country that compared what respondents predicted for a fictional person with what they themselves later did, or a systematic review of vignette measures of social norms. Searches covered reviews of social norm measures in low- and middle-income countries, vignette experiments that vary what others do and approve of, vignette experiments in developing countries, and checks of vignette answers against behavior.

What depends on the place is whether respondents are used to reasoning about a made-up person, whether the story reads as a home like theirs, who must be named in the cues, and whether a statement about what most people do is heard as an expectation or as news about the practice.

## Replication

The finding that respondents' predictions shift with what a story says others do has field support from Mali, and vignette studies have produced interpretable answers in Benin, Rwanda, Ghana, and Zambia. That is support for the method working as a survey tool. Whether a shift in predictions for a fictional person matches a shift in what respondents themselves would do has not been tested for norms; the method assumes it. Measures of norms in general are not standardized.

## What to check locally

1. Whether respondents will reason about a made-up person. Some answer "it depends on the person"; test the story with a few respondents first.
2. Whether the story reads as a home like the respondent's own. If not, the answers describe a stranger.
3. Whether respondents answer for the fictional person by thinking of themselves, which the method needs. Ask them after the questions.
4. Whether "most people do not" is heard as an expectation or as news that the practice is unsafe.
5. Whether the interview can be held out of hearing of the people the cues name.
6. Whether the practice is harmful, illegal, or stigmatized, which sends the wording to a local researcher and an ethics reviewer first.
