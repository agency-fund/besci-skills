# Evidence: Track changes to a tested intervention

## Sources

- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: the component table and its two variation columns. The framework says interventions "require a theoretical deconstruction into components and then agreement about permissible and prohibited variation in the delivery of those components", which lets delivery vary across contexts "yet maintain the integrity of the core intervention components". It names adapting an existing intervention "to a new population, to a new setting" or to other outcomes as a source of interventions, and allows flexible implementation "provided that the key functions of the programme are maintained, and that the adaptations made are clearly understood". Its example, the ASSIST school smoking-prevention program, had a manual that "identified critical components and other components that could be adapted or dropped".
- Wiltsey Stirman, S., Baumann, A. A., & Miller, C. J. (2019). The FRAME: An expanded framework for reporting adaptations and modifications to evidence-based interventions. *Implementation Science*, 14, 58. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13012-019-0898-y Supports: the eight fields of the change log. The framework reports "(1) when and how in the implementation process the modification was made, (2) whether the modification was planned/proactive (i.e., an adaptation) or unplanned/reactive, (3) who determined that the modification should be made, (4) what is modified, (5) at what level of delivery the modification is made, (6) type or nature of context or content-level modifications, (7) the extent to which the modification is fidelity-consistent, and (8) the reasons for the modification", the reasons split into the goal and the contextual factors behind it. Fidelity-consistent modifications are "those that preserve core elements of a treatment that are needed for the intervention to be effective"; the framework adds an "unknown" code for when no theory or evidence shows whether an element is core. Its phases run from pre-implementation through implementation to scale-up and sustainment. The authors note that providers "may not recognize that they are modifying the interventions", which is why the log asks frontline staff and reads monitoring data. The skill's list of the nature of a change (added, removed, shortened, substituted, reordered, tailored) is a short version of its own, not the framework's full list.
- Moore, G., Campbell, M., Copeland, L., Craig, P., Movsisyan, A., Hoddinott, P., Littlecott, H., O'Cathain, A., Pfadenhauer, L., Rehfuess, E., Segrott, J., Hawe, P., Kee, F., Couturiaux, D., Hallingberg, B., & Evans, R. (2021). Adapting interventions to new contexts—the ADAPT guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n1679. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n1679 Supports: the steps for a move to a new context. The guidance has four steps: assess the rationale for the intervention and its fit with the new context; plan and undertake adaptations; plan and undertake piloting and evaluation; implement and maintain the adapted intervention at scale, with stakeholders involved throughout. It says that what matters in comparing contexts is "the presence of contextual contingencies for successful implementation and for the intervention to function as intended in terms of change mechanisms", recommends that teams "document and classify responsive adaptations", and treats an intervention whose mechanisms have "been substantially changed" as a new intervention.
- Walton, G. M., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Wise interventions: Psychological remedies for social and personal problems. *Psychological Review*, 125(5), 617–655. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000115 Supports: the assumption column. The article calls it "imperative to develop protocols that explicate the assumptions behind intervention materials effective in past contexts and discuss how they can be revised for new settings". Its example: asking citizens how important it was to "be a voter" raised turnout before two major elections, and did not in low-profile primaries, where being a voter "does not carry significant identity stakes".
- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2010). *Nine steps for developing a scaling-up strategy*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241500319 Supports: adapting without losing what makes the program work. Its question "Which components will need local adaptation to be relevant for changes in local context?" comes with the action "Identify needed local adaptations while ensuring the essence of the innovation remains intact".
- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2011). *Beginning with the end in mind*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241502320 Supports: keeping a record. Recommendation 11 asks the project team to "document the changes in the package of interventions that are being made and the reasons why".
- Fernandez, M. E., Ruiter, R. A. C., Markham, C. M., & Kok, G. (2019). Intervention Mapping: Theory- and evidence-based health promotion program planning: Perspective and examples. *Frontiers in Public Health*, 7, 209. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2019.00209 Supports: adaptation as a named planning task. The overview names "IM for adaptation of evidence-based programs in new settings" as a direction of the framework.
- West, R., Michie, S., Atkins, L., Chadwick, P., & Lorencatto, F. (2019). *Achieving behaviour change: A guide for local government and partners*. Public Health England. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5e7b4e85d3bf7f133c923435/PHEBI_Achieving_Behaviour_Change_Local_Government.pdf Supports: adaptation as a named planning task. The guide, built on the Behaviour Change Wheel, describes "Adapting interventions" as a scenario, including adapting "an existing intervention for a different population, setting or behaviour".

The rule that core components are listed before the changes are read, and the judgment of the changes taken together, are this skill's own.

What depends on the place is which components are core. The reporting framework's own example: a part that teaches basic health literacy may be essential for one population and less needed for another.

## Replication

Not applicable. None of the sources tests whether recording and judging changes this way leads to better outcomes than not doing so. The fidelity judgment in each row is a structured opinion, as good as the component table behind it.

## What to check locally

1. Which components do their job in this population: ask the people who deliver and receive it, not only the designers.
2. What frontline staff have already changed, from monitoring data and by asking them directly.
3. Whether any change touches clinical, safety, or legal content.
4. Whether a change made to save money has a cheaper form that does the same job and could be tested first.
