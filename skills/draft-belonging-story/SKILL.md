## What it does

Drafts a set of stories from older peers that present early difficulty in a new setting as something most newcomers pass through.

**What this adds:** writes no stories where the group is harassed or excluded; takes stories only from real older peers of the same group, each with a difficulty, the doubt it raised, and what changed; labels any story it writes itself "illustration, not for delivery"; includes the task in which newcomers advise the next intake; and says the trials are from US colleges.

The exercise is Walton and Cohen's social-belonging intervention, one of Walton and Wilson's wise interventions: short exercises that change how people interpret their situation.

## When to use it

### Use it when
- Newcomers' own words name the question "do people like me belong here?", and what the setting offers them is known
- Someone asks for "a belonging intervention" or "testimonials from older students"
- A team is about to write the stories itself

### Do not use it when
- Nobody has established which question newcomers are asking; use `identify-wise-intervention-target`
- Their words are about ability ("I am not a math person"); use `adapt-growth-mindset-message`
- They feel judged for who they are or what they lack; use `draft-values-affirmation`

## Before you start

Ask these before drafting the stories, unless already answered.

1. **Required.** The behavior (who, what, when), the transition, and, in newcomers' words with counts, evidence that they read early difficulty as "people like me do not belong here".
2. **Required.** Who in the setting includes the group, and any report of harassment or exclusion, with who was asked. Facts, not plans.
3. **Required.** Older peers of the same group who can be interviewed, or their accounts if already collected.
4. Optional. When, in what form, and by whom the stories are delivered.

If 1 or 3 is missing, ask and stop. If 2 is unknown, write the collection plan only. If 4 is missing, assume one session in the first weeks, led by older peers.

## What it draws on

- Walton, G. M., & Cohen, G. L. (2011). A brief social-belonging intervention improves academic and health outcomes of minority students. *Science*, 331(6023), 1447–1451. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1198364 Supports: the message, adversity framed as "common and transient".
- Murphy, M. C., Gopalan, M., Carter, E. R., et al. (2020). A customized belonging intervention improves retention of socially disadvantaged students at a broad-access university. *Science Advances*, 6(29). https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aba4677 Supports: steps 3 to 6. Newcomers read stories from the university's own older students, then wrote to a future student.
- Walton, G. M., Murphy, M. C., Logel, C., et al. (2023). Where and with whom does a brief social-belonging intervention promote progress in college? *Science*, 380(6644), 499–505. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ade4420 Supports: step 2. Among 26,911 students at 22 US institutions, it worked only where students' groups had real opportunities to belong.
- Walton, G. M., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Wise interventions: Psychological remedies for social and personal problems. *Psychological Review*, 125(5), 617–655. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000115 Supports: the framework.

**Where the evidence comes from:** Every large trial is from colleges in the United States. The first trial outside North America, with 86 first-year students, had mixed results, and no field trial in a low- or middle-income country was found. Check locally which group line matters and whether the setting includes the group.

**Replication status:** Replicated in the United States, on condition that the group has real opportunities to belong; untested at scale elsewhere.

## How to do it

1. **Confirm the question** from the newcomers' words. If they are about ability or being judged, say so and stop.
2. **Check the setting, as a gate.** If the group is harassed or excluded, write no stories and say what must change first: a story would ask people to distrust a reading that is correct.
3. **Plan the collection:** six to twelve older peers of the same group, each asked what was hard in the first weeks, what they thought it meant about people like them, and what changed. Tellers consent and approve the edit.
4. **Select and edit.** Keep a story only if it has a concrete early difficulty, the doubt it raised in the teller's words, what changed and when, and a sign that others felt the same. Cut any that promises what the setting lacks. Mark every edit.
5. **Invent nothing.** If no accounts exist yet, write one story labeled "illustration, not for delivery" to show the shape.
6. **Write the advice task:** newcomers write a letter to a future newcomer who doubts they belong. People come to hold an idea by explaining it ("saying is believing"). Offer a spoken version, and say truthfully where the letters go.
7. **Fix delivery and outcome:** once, before the first setback, with newcomers cast as helping the next intake; persistence or completion on a named date, against a comparison group. A belonging survey shows only that the stories landed.
8. **State the record** and promise no effect size.

## Where it goes wrong

- **An illustration that gets used.** A story written to show the shape is easy to print and hand out. Presented as real, it is a deception.
- **The star alumni.** Institutions nominate their most successful graduates, whose stories tell a newcomer that others found it easy.
- **Stories in place of inclusion.** They cost nothing, so they get offered where the fix is a warden who acts.
- **Quoting the first study.** It halved a gap among 92 students; the large trial found an effect only under a condition.

## When to bring in a specialist

Tell the user to consult a safeguarding lead or the institution's leadership, with a local qualitative researcher, when:
- The group reports harassment or discrimination; the fix is institutional
- The group is defined by a stigmatized status (ethnicity, caste, religion, disability); a published story can expose its teller
- Newcomers are minors or recently displaced; a counselor should review the stories
- The rollout is institution-wide or national; an evaluator should pre-register a test first

Bring them: the newcomers' words, the check of the setting, and the stories.
