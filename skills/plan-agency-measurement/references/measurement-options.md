# Choosing measures (steps 2 to 5)

Use this to give each focus cell of the 2x2 agency framework a measure on the platform and one off it, each tied to a moment. The instruments named here are candidates to look up, adapt, and test. None has been shown to work in a chat on WhatsApp or a similar channel in a low-income setting. Full citations are in `evidence.md`.

## On the platform and off it

| | On-platform | Off-platform |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Short in-app or in-chat questions, conversation logs, digital traces (taps, edits, timing), task behavior | Longer phone or in-person surveys with validated measures, observation, interviews, records, work products (a pitch, a farm plan) |
| Strengths | Cheap, in the moment, large samples, behavior rather than recall | Room for multi-item scales and context; outcomes the tool cannot see |
| Limits | Short; answered mostly by engaged users; asked by the tool about itself; blind to life off the tool | Costly; smaller samples; recall; interviewer effects |

Rules for an in-app check:

- Three items or fewer, asked inside the chat right after the user finishes a task, not as a pop-up (Wu et al., 2025).
- Keep it short. Among 163 students in an experience-sampling study, longer questionnaires raised burden and lowered data quality; more frequent prompts did not (Eisele et al., 2022).
- A chat can be a sound survey channel. Chatbot surveys drew less satisficing (answering with minimal effort) than web forms in one experiment (Kim, Lee & Gweon, 2019), and among teachers in the Philippines chatbot answers matched phone interviews on several measures of quality (Beam, 2022).
- Check a one-item pulse against a multi-item version run off the platform. In one intensive longitudinal study, single items correlated .24 to .61 with multi-item measures and kept predictive validity in 27 of 29 models (Song et al., 2023).

## Candidates by cell

### With respect to the end goal, internally experienced (thoughts and feelings)

- **Self-efficacy for the task the tool supports,** not general confidence. Agricultural self-efficacy and livelihoods self-efficacy scales were tested in nine surveys in six African countries (Clingain, Donald & Hernandez-de-Benito, 2026). Use the 10-item General Self-Efficacy Scale, if at all, as a background measure: one study found it had one dimension in 25 countries (Scholz et al., 2002); another found several dimensions and items that behaved differently by country in 26 (Villegas Barahona et al., 2018).
- **Goal setting:** the goal-setting capacity scale from the same six-country work, or the user's own goal in their words, recorded at the first session so later progress is judged against it.
- **Perceived control:** the short locus of control scale from the same work. Its internal reliability was weaker than the other scales'.
- **Why the user pursues the goal:** the Relative Autonomy Index, which asks how far a person acts under coercion or social pressure rather than from her own values. It was used with a nationally representative sample in Chad (Vaz, Pratley & Alkire, 2016).
- **Hope:** the 6-item State Hope Scale measures belief in one's capacity to start and sustain action toward goals and to find routes to them, and it moves with events (Snyder et al., 1996). Hope has also been measured without self-report, from children's self-portraits in Indonesia (Glewwe, Ross & Wydick, 2018).
- Felt confidence can rise while capability does not. Pair every measure in this cell with one of what the user does, from the externally expressed row.

### With respect to the digital tool, internally experienced

- **Confidence in oneself and in the tool,** asked after a task. Among 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking and higher self-confidence with more (Lee et al., 2025). A widening gap between the two is a warning sign.
- **Felt freedom to question or reject advice:** one item, right after the tool gives a recommendation.
- **Trust and dependence scales:** a trust-in-automation scale built from 12 factors (Jian, Bisantz & Drury, 2000); a one-factor scale of dependence on AI, from 528 university students (Morales-García et al., 2024); an 8-item chatbot dependence scale (Zhang et al., 2025). Check who each was built with before using it here. Self-reported use of digital media tracks logs only moderately, and scales of problematic use track them less (Parry et al., 2021), so take use from the logs.
- **Why the user follows the tool:** reason items in the style of the Relative Autonomy Index, written as drafted examples ("I follow the advice because I would get in trouble otherwise / because others expect me to / because I agree with it"). They separate delegation the user endorses from compliance.
- **AI literacy, as a moderator:** the AI self-efficacy and AI self-management facets of the Meta AI Literacy Scale, built with 300 German-speaking adults (Carolus et al., 2023).

### With respect to the end goal, externally expressed (choices and actions)

- **Progress against the user's own goal,** from logs where it is visible and from follow-up surveys or records where it is not.
- **Unassisted outcomes:** performance with the tool removed. In a field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school students, an unguarded AI tutor raised practice grades by 48%; once access was removed, those students scored 17% below students who never had it (Bastani et al., 2025).
- **Rated work or observation:** a pitch, a farm plan, or classroom behavior, rated against written anchors by trained raters and linked where possible to an outcome the team trusts.
- **Coded processes in conversation logs:** setting and revising goals, weighing options, adjusting after setbacks, following Bandura's account of agency as intentionality, forethought, self-reactiveness, and self-reflectiveness (Bandura, 2001). Experimental; see the protocol in `validation.md`.
- **Decision-making items** ("who decided ...?") where the goal involves household decisions. Being the decision-maker signals agency only if the person wants a say (Donald & Hernandez-de-Benito, 2021), so ask that too. Small differences in how these indicators were built changed how women ranked and whether programs appeared to have effects in Ecuador, Uganda, and Yemen (Peterman et al., 2021); spouses in Bangladesh disagreed systematically about who decides (Ambler et al., 2021). In Haryana, India, five questions chosen against coded qualitative interviews tracked those interviews as well as the full set of candidate questions (Jayachandran, Biradavolu & Cooper, 2023). These questions can put respondents at risk; see the skill's specialist section.

### With respect to the digital tool, externally expressed

- **Appropriate reliance:** where a task has a right answer, record the user's answer, then show the tool's, then record the final answer. Score both relying on correct advice and turning down incorrect advice (Schemmer et al., 2023).
- **Asking for the answer versus working the problem:** the share of turns that ask the tool for the answer or the finished product. Students with an unguarded tutor used it as a "crutch" and then did worse on their own (Bastani et al., 2025). A related split, augmentation versus automation, has been applied to millions of conversations (Handa et al., 2025).
- **Questioning, adapting, or rejecting advice:** turns where the user pushes back, edits a suggestion, or chooses otherwise.
- **Disempowerment potential,** for tools that give value-laden advice: conversations that risk distorting a user's view of reality, leading to value judgments that are not their own, or prompting actions out of line with their values (Sharma et al., 2026, a preprint).
- **Observation or interviews** on how users work with the tool, including off it: copying text into other apps, sharing answers with family.

## Moments in the user journey

| Moment | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| First session | The user's own goal, in their words; baseline self-efficacy | First-session novelty |
| Right after a decision, critique, or recommendation | Felt control; agreeing or disagreeing with the tool; confidence | Asking too often; the tool asking about itself |
| After a setback (a failed attempt, bad weather, a missed visit) | Persistence; perceived control under pressure | Small numbers; sensitive moments |
| When the tool is unavailable or removed | Unassisted capability; dependence | Needs planning into the program |
| End of a program cycle | Multi-item scales; goal attainment; records | The least engaged have dropped out |

## Reducing reporting bias

A tool that promotes agency invites users to report more of it.

- Ask about the task and the person, not the product. Questions that ask users to rate the tool carry halo effects: users who like it report better outcomes whatever changed (Wu et al., 2025).
- Ask about specific, recent situations, without signaling what the study hopes to find. When instructions named the study's focus, reports were biased; when they did not, reports matched records (Brenner & DeLamater, 2016).
- Use anchoring vignettes, short stories about hypothetical people that respondents rate on the same scale as themselves, to detect and correct differences in how groups use a scale (King et al., 2004).
- Hold enumerators constant across arms, and let people answer privately where possible. Response patterns and enumerator interactions distorted personality measures in face-to-face surveys in 23 countries (Laajaj et al., 2019).
- Do not assume an indirect method is more accurate. In Peru, a list experiment found no difference from direct questions about violence (Agüero & Frisancho, 2022).

## Digital traces: name the error before trusting the signal

Following the total error framework for digital traces (Sen et al., 2021), ask of every log signal:

- Who is not on the platform, and who on it never produces this signal?
- Can the platform's design produce this behavior at all? A chatbot that never asks for the user's answer first cannot show self-checking.
- Does one account stand for one person? Shared phones are common.
- Was the signal labeled by a model, and has that labeling been checked?

## Search queries

When a published instrument is needed and the source is not open, give the user a query with four parts: the construct and its cell, the population and language, the domain, and what the result is for.

- "The full item wording and response format of [instrument] ([authors, year]). Quote the items from the original paper or its supplement, give the link, and say whether the instrument is free to use."
- "Validated scales measuring [construct] for [population] in [domain]: validation sample and setting, number of items, response format, example items, and known limits in [language]. Cite sources."
- "How has [construct] been measured from behavior, observation, or conversation logs in [domain], and how were those measures validated? Cite sources."
- "How have studies measured dependence on, or over-reliance on, [type of AI tool] among [population]? Which behaviors and survey items were used? Cite sources."

Many published scales are licensed. Note the terms where the source states them, and quote a few example items rather than a full licensed scale.
