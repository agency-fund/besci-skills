# Evidence: Check whether a pilot effect will survive scale-up

## Sources

- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2010). *Nine steps for developing a scaling-up strategy*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241500319 Supports: comparing the pilot with routine delivery. Step 1 assesses the attributes that make an innovation scalable, with questions that include "Has the innovation been tested in the type of setting where it will be scaled up?" and "Were major additional human or financial resources and commodities needed to introduce the innovation?". The guide says that "when innovations have been tested in a setting and with a level of resources that differ significantly from the settings where they are to be scaled up, additional testing under realistic operating conditions is needed", and closes step 1 with the question "Is it still appropriate to proceed with plans for scaling up?". It also warns against scaling "before its effectiveness and feasibility have been fully established".
- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2011). *Beginning with the end in mind*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241502320 Supports: the plus, minus, and more-information marks, and the first test. The guide's checklist for assessing the potential scalability of a project has 23 questions grouped under its 12 recommendations, each answered yes (+), no (–), or more information or action needed. The guide says the checklist "should not be used mechanically" and that some items may act as "deal-breakers". Recommendation 7 is to test the innovation "under the routine operating conditions and existing resource constraints of the health system", if necessary in two stages: "proof of concept", then "proof of implementation, utilizing routinely available resources".
- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: the rows on demand, cost, who delivers, how it reaches people, and the strength and reach of the evidence. The guide summarizes the factors in rolling out at a larger scale as SCALE: Sponsorship, Cost/benefit, Accountability, Logistics, and Evidence. Its example: letters hand-delivered in a trial and then sent through an unreliable postal service will reach fewer people, so the average effect will be smaller.
- Walton, G. M., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Wise interventions: Psychological remedies for social and personal problems. *Psychological Review*, 125(5), 617–655. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000115 Supports: the rows on what is delivered and how it reaches people. The article says scaling requires "(a) expertise in the meanings targeted and techniques to alter them; (b) expertise in the setting and population at hand, and ways of adapting the intervention as necessary; and (c) effective and reliable means of large-scale delivery". It also expects an intervention delivered in a scalable way to a large, diverse sample to produce smaller effects than one tailored and hand-delivered to a more homogeneous population, which is why the assessment does not carry the pilot's number forward.
- Milat, A., Lee, K., Conte, K., Grunseit, A., Wolfenden, L., van Nassau, F., Orr, N., Sreeram, P., & Bauman, A. (2020). Intervention Scalability Assessment Tool: A decision support tool for health policy makers and implementers. *Health Research Policy and Systems*, 18, 1. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12961-019-0494-2 Supports: the three verdicts and the rule against a total score. The tool has five domains for setting the scene (the problem, the intervention, the strategic and political context, evidence of effectiveness, costs and benefits) and five for implementation (fidelity and adaptation, reach and acceptability, delivery setting and workforce, implementation infrastructure, sustainability). Questions are rated 0 to 3, and the authors say "there is no 'total' or summary score", on purpose, because not all domains are equally important in every context. It ends with a recommendation that the intervention is recommended for scale-up, is promising but needs further information, or does not yet merit scale-up.
- Al-Ubaydli, O., List, J. A., & Suskind, D. (2020). 2017 Klein Lecture: The science of using science: Toward an understanding of the threats to scalability. *International Economic Review*, 61(4), 1387–1409. https://doi.org/10.1111/iere.12476 Supports: the rows on the strength of the effect, who is reached, and the conditions of delivery. The authors name three elements of the scale-up problem: when evidence becomes actionable, properties of the population, and properties of the situation. Abstract only.
- Bold, T., Kimenyi, M., Mwabu, G., Ng'ang'a, A., & Sandefur, J. (2018). Experimental evidence on scaling up education reforms in Kenya. *Journal of Public Economics*, 168, 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2018.08.007 Supports: treating a change of implementer as untested. Abstract only.
- Banerjee, A., Banerji, R., Berry, J., Duflo, E., Kannan, H., Mukerji, S., Shotland, M., & Walton, M. (2017). From proof of concept to scalable policies: Challenges and solutions, with an application. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 31(4), 73–102. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.31.4.73 Supports: testing under the system that will deliver at scale. Abstract only.
- Vivalt, E. (2020). How much can we generalize from impact evaluations? *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 18(6), 3045–3089. https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvaa019 Supports: the row on who delivers. Abstract only.

The seven rows as a set, the rule that an untested difference counts as a minus, and the definition of each verdict are this skill's own, assembled from the sources above.

## Replication

Not applicable to the procedure: no source tests whether teams that assess scalability this way scale more successfully. The row on who delivers has the strongest support, from a trial that ran the same program under two implementers in Kenya and from a pooled analysis of impact evaluations, both read in abstract only.

## What to check locally

1. Who will deliver at scale, and whether their selection, training, supervision, and pay match the pilot's.
2. Which extras the pilot had (research staff, supervision visits, phone credit, a call-back) and whether each is in the budget at scale.
3. Whether the organization that would deliver at scale has asked for the program, in writing.
4. Whether the people reached at scale include groups the pilot did not.
