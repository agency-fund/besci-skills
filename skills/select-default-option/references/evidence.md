# Evidence: Choose what people get by default

## Sources

- Jachimowicz, J. M., Duncan, S., Weber, E. U., & Johnson, E. J. (2019). When and why defaults influence decisions: a meta-analysis of default effects. *Behavioural Public Policy*, 3(2), 159–186. https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2018.43 Supports: the mechanism line, and the warning that effects vary and can reverse. The pooled effect across 58 data sets from 55 studies (73,675 participants) is d = 0.68 (95% CI 0.53 to 0.83), where d is the difference between groups in standard deviations. Nearly all the variation between studies is real difference (I² = 98%), and two data sets show a significant negative effect. Effects were larger in consumer settings and smaller in environmental ones. Defaults working through endorsement or endowment had larger effects; ease did not explain differences between studies, which the authors think may reflect how it was measured.
- Blumenstock, J., Callen, M., & Ghani, T. (2018). Why do defaults affect behavior? Experimental evidence from Afghanistan. *American Economic Review*, 108(10), 2868–2901. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20171676 Supports: step 1. Abstract only.
- Somville, V., & Vandewalle, L. (2018). Saving by default: Evidence from a field experiment in rural India. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 10(3), 39–66. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20160547 Supports: checking what happens when the default ends. Abstract only.
- Brune, L., Giné, X., Goldberg, J., & Yang, D. (2017). Savings defaults and payment delays for cash transfers: Field experimental evidence from Malawi. *Journal of Development Economics*, 129, 1–13. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2017.06.001 Supports: the harm check. Abstract of the working-paper version only.
- OECD (2019). *Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit*. https://doi.org/10.1787/9ea76a8f-en Supports: the opt-out rule and the ethics check. Its guidance for choosing a strategy includes "Prioritise transparency", "Offer a way out", and "Ensure the policy intervention serves the public interest".
- The Behavioural Insights Team (2024). *EAST: Four simple ways to apply behavioural insights* (revised and updated edition). https://bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/BIT-EAST.pdf Supports: establishing what people prefer, and how to seek their permission, before setting a default.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|

| [Blumenstock, Callen & Ghani (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20171676) | Randomized field experiment; abstract only | Afghanistan | Default enrollment in a salary-linked savings account raised participation by 40 percentage points, as much as a 50% match. The authors trace it to present bias and the effort of thinking through the choice |
| [Somville & Vandewalle (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20160547) | Randomized field experiment; abstract only | India | Weekly payments made into a bank account, not in cash, raised account savings by 131% within three months. Once everyone was paid in cash again, savings no longer differed |
| [Brune et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2017.06.001) | Randomized field experiment with 474 households; abstract of the working paper only | Malawi | A one-time payment deposited into a bank account led to higher balances for several weeks. It did not change how much households spent or on what |
| [Reñosa et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2021-006237) | Systematic review of 48 studies of nudges for vaccination; full text | 46 of 48 in high- or upper-middle-income countries | The three studies of changed defaults were all run in the United Kingdom or the United States |

Outside wealthy Western countries the field evidence is about savings and payments. The scan found no field trial of a default in health or consent in a low- or middle-income country. What differs by place is who has the standing to set a default and whether people can afford to leave money or a service where the default puts it.

## Replication

The direction replicates across many studies. The size does not: the pooled estimate hides studies with no effect and two with the opposite effect. The Malawi study is a caution about what a default changes: balances rose, and spending did not.

## What to check locally

1. Whether people want the option, measured and not assumed.
2. Whether the party setting the default is trusted with the thing it moves: wages, records, consent.
3. Whether anyone needs the money or service immediately, and what the default costs them.
4. Whether opting out is as easy for the least confident person as for the most.
