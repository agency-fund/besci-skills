# Which kinds of intervention can address which kind of barrier

The lookup table for steps 2 and 3: six kinds of barrier, nine kinds of intervention, and the links between them, from Michie, van Stralen and West (2011), Tables 1 and 2. https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-5908-6-42

## The six kinds of barrier

The paper sorts what a behavior needs into capability, opportunity, and motivation (the COM-B model), each split in two.

| Kind of barrier | What is missing | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Physical capability | The physical skill or strength to do it | Cannot work the handpump |
| Psychological capability | The knowledge, understanding, or mental skill to do it | Cannot work out how much of the day's takings is profit |
| Physical opportunity | What the environment affords: time, money, places, objects | The agent's kiosk closes before the market does |
| Social opportunity | What the people and customs around the person allow | Neighbors ask to borrow from anyone seen with cash |
| Reflective motivation | Evaluations and plans: what the person believes and intends | Believes the lender never enforces the deadline |
| Automatic motivation | Emotions, impulses, and habits | Hands over the day's cash at home without thinking |

## The nine kinds of intervention

The paper calls these intervention functions. Definitions follow its Table 1.

| Kind | Definition |
|---|---|
| Education | Increasing knowledge or understanding |
| Persuasion | Using communication to induce positive or negative feelings or to stimulate action |
| Incentivization | Creating an expectation of reward |
| Coercion | Creating an expectation of punishment or cost |
| Training | Imparting skills |
| Restriction | Using rules to reduce the opportunity to engage in the behavior, or to increase the behavior by reducing the opportunity to engage in competing behaviors |
| Environmental restructuring | Changing the physical or social context |
| Modeling | Providing an example for people to aspire to or imitate |
| Enablement | Increasing means or reducing barriers to increase capability or opportunity, beyond what education, training, and environmental restructuring do |

The same rows as lists:

- **Physical capability:** training, enablement
- **Psychological capability:** education, training, enablement
- **Reflective motivation:** education, persuasion, incentivization, coercion
- **Automatic motivation:** persuasion, incentivization, coercion, environmental restructuring, modeling, enablement
- **Physical opportunity:** restriction, environmental restructuring, enablement
- **Social opportunity:** restriction, environmental restructuring, enablement

## The authors' later, coarser table

West, R., Michie, S., Atkins, L., Chadwick, P., & Lorencatto, F. (2019). *Achieving behaviour change: A guide for local government and partners*. Public Health England. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/behaviour-change-guide-for-local-government-and-partners

Table 5 of this guide maps the nine kinds to capability, opportunity, and motivation without splitting each in two, and calls the mapping indicative. It adds three links the 2011 table does not have:

- **Training** for opportunity and for motivation
- **Modeling** for opportunity

A kind that is eligible only through one of these links may be listed in step 3, marked "later table".

## Reading it

- The links are the authors' judgment, reached by discussion and by testing against examples. A link says a kind of intervention could change that kind of barrier in principle, not that it will.
- Coercion is wider than force. The 2019 guide counts anything with an unattractive outcome, including social disapproval and a higher price.
- A default is environmental restructuring, and so is a prompt placed where the behavior happens. A message about what other people do can be persuasion, modeling, or coercion, depending on whether it makes the behavior attractive, shows an example to copy, or signals disapproval.
