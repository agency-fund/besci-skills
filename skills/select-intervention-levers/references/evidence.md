# Evidence: Choose interventions for a confirmed barrier

## Sources

- Michie, S., van Stralen, M. M., & West, R. (2011). The behaviour change wheel: A new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions. *Implementation Science*, 6, 42. https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-5908-6-42 Supports: the six kinds of barrier, the nine kinds of intervention (Table 1), and the table linking them (Table 2), reproduced in `function-matrix.md`. Read in full. The authors built the links through "an iterative process of discussion and testing against specific examples and counter-examples", and write that the most important test of the framework will be whether it gives a more efficient way of choosing interventions. The paper does not run that test.
- Michie, S., Atkins, L., & West, R. (2014). *The Behaviour Change Wheel: A Guide to Designing Interventions*. Silverback Publishing. Supports: choosing the kind of intervention before the technique, and the APEASE criteria. The book is not open. The steps and criteria here were checked against an open guide by its authors: West, R., Michie, S., Atkins, L., Chadwick, P., & Lorencatto, F. (2019). *Achieving behaviour change: A guide for local government and partners*. Public Health England. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/behaviour-change-guide-for-local-government-and-partners Read in full. That guide defines the six criteria, says to "drop or amend any interventions that fall down on any one of the criteria" (step 6), suggests a question mark where there is not enough information to judge (the "?" score), and prints the coarser table that `function-matrix.md` calls the later table.
- Michie, S., Richardson, M., Johnston, M., Abraham, C., Francis, J., Hardeman, W., Eccles, M. P., Cane, J., & Wood, C. E. (2013). The behavior change technique taxonomy (v1) of 93 hierarchically clustered techniques: Building an international consensus for the reporting of behavior change interventions. *Annals of Behavioral Medicine*, 46(1), 81–95. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6 Supports: naming one technique in step 7. Abstract only. Fourteen experts rated labels and definitions of 124 techniques, and 18 grouped them, giving 93 techniques in 16 groups. When six researchers coded 85 intervention descriptions, 23 of the 26 techniques that occurred at least five times reached an adjusted kappa of 0.60 or above (kappa measures agreement beyond chance).
- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: scoring practicability against what the implementer controls. Read in full. Its Solution stage links each idea to a barrier, then narrows the list by potential impact and feasibility; the feasibility questions include budget, whether stakeholders will accept the idea, and whether "your organisation [has] the authority to implement the intervention".

Other process guides name the same task, which is why the library carries it:

- OECD (2019). *Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit*. https://doi.org/10.1787/9ea76a8f-en Stage 3 has three steps: identify strategies that match the behavioral analysis, conceptualize an intervention, and screen it for ethics, feasibility, and costs. Read in part.
- World Bank (2015). *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0342-0 Table 11.1, "Different obstacles may require different intervention design", sets the obstacles to home water chlorination against the designs that could overcome each. That table read; the chapter in part.
- Jang, C., Koki, E., Nyaga, R., Okafor, A., Singh, J., Vang, A., & Wendel, S. (2024). *The Busara toolkit: leveraging behavioral science for development*. Busara Groundwork No. 10. https://doi.org/10.62372/WQSB6195 Its design phase uses lookup tables that match techniques to kinds of barrier, one of them for COM-B. Read in full, except figure text.
- The TOPS Program (2017). *Designing for Behavior Change: A Practical Field Guide*. Activities are chosen to address what the barrier study found, and must pass three criteria: relevance to those findings, feasibility, and receptivity of the audience. Read in full.
- Fernandez, M. E., Ruiter, R. A. C., Markham, C. M., & Kok, G. (2019). Intervention Mapping: Theory- and evidence-based health promotion program planning: Perspective and examples. *Frontiers in Public Health*, 7, 209. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2019.00209 Step 3 selects change methods for the determinants to be changed, then turns each into a practical application that fits the group and the setting. Read in full.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: saying whether the team's own idea is in the row, giving each kind a concrete form before scoring it, and marking a taxonomy label given from memory.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026. The skill is a selection procedure, so this table records where its sources come from and where the wheel has been used.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Michie, van Stralen & West (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-5908-6-42) | Framework paper; full text | United Kingdom | Built from 19 earlier frameworks. Two of the authors classified the parts of an English tobacco strategy and of English obesity guidance, agreeing on 88% and 79% of items. Two policy experts agreed with the resulting standard on 85% and 75% |
| [Michie et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6) | Consensus exercise; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | 93 techniques in 16 groups; agreement between coders was adequate for 23 of the 26 most frequent techniques |
| [West et al. (2019)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/behaviour-change-guide-for-local-government-and-partners) | Practitioner guide; full text | Written for English local government | No test of the method is reported. It calls its own table "indicative" |
| [Cuevas et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czab021) | Systematic review of 43 articles on antibiotic use; abstract only | Low- and middle-income countries, most in Sub-Saharan Africa or East Asia and the Pacific | Used the wheel to sort what interventions contained. Education for providers was the most common strategy, and worked best alongside training or other enabling measures. The review classifies interventions; it does not test the wheel as a way to choose them |

The scan looked for a study comparing interventions chosen with the table against interventions chosen another way, in any country. None was found. Searches: "Behaviour Change Wheel intervention development low- and middle-income countries systematic review"; "are interventions developed using the Behaviour Change Wheel more effective"; "behaviour change technique taxonomy cross-cultural applicability low- and middle-income countries".

The table does not depend on the population. The scores do: what is affordable, who must give permission, what the audience accepts, and who would be left out are local facts.

## Replication

Not applicable. The table is a judgment, not an effect. What has been measured is whether people apply the wheel's labels alike (75% to 88% agreement in the 2011 paper) and whether coders recognize the same techniques (adequate for most of the frequent ones in the 2013 paper). Whether two teams given the same barrier would select the same intervention has not been tested.

## What to check locally

1. Who has to give permission for each eligible kind, asked before scoring practicability.
2. What the audience says it would accept, asked of members of the audience and not of staff.
3. Who would be left out by the selected technique: people without a phone, without the fee, or without the standing to use it.
4. Whether anything like the selected technique has been tried nearby, and what happened.
