# Evidence: Find out whose opinion really matters

## Sources

- Bicchieri, C., & Penn Social Norms Training and Consulting Group (2016). *Why Do People Do What They Do? A Social Norms Manual for Viet Nam, Indonesia and the Philippines*. UNICEF Office of Research – Innocenti. https://repository.upenn.edu/pennsong/12 Supports: the three designs, the per-role question, and the definition. The manual lists the full network, snowball, and ego-centric approaches "in descending order in term of cost but also reliability". The full network approach "can only work for smaller communities"; the snowball approach "strikes a nice balance" but can miss a group cut off from the starting points, so "careful choice of starting points" matters; the ego-centric approach is the cheapest, the least reliable, and "potentially most useful in a large city". It suggests asking, for each member of the network, "If ... gave you different advice, would you stop doing ...?", and says a reference network "is not just those individuals who happen to be geographically close". Sections 1, 3, and 5 read.
- Institute for Reproductive Health, Georgetown University (2020). *Social Norms Exploration Tool*. https://www.irh.org/social-norms-exploration/ Supports: how the interviews are run and counted. Phase 2 "identifies Reference Groups — those who matter to individuals and the way they behave", through one-to-one interviews of five to ten minutes with four or five questions for each behavior. Its advice: "It's not necessary to record names of people; rather, record what roles they have". Analysis adds up how often each type of person is named, and "two or fewer Reference Groups per behavior is recommended". Programs in "over 15 settings" used it between 2016 and 2019. Phases 1 and 2 and the annexes read.
- The TOPS Program (2017). *Designing for Behavior Change: A Practical Field Guide*. https://web.archive.org/web/20180329030310/http://fsnnetwork.org/sites/default/files/designing_for_behavior_change_a_practical_field_guide.pdf Supports: asking who approves and who disapproves. Step 7, "Identify the Influencing Group(s)": answers to "Who Approves?" and "Who Disapproves?" may reveal groups that "can either support or prevent the Priority Group from adopting Behaviors. They are usually very close to the Priority Group, such as someone's mother-in-law, or spouse."
- UNICEF (2022). *Social & Behaviour Change at UNICEF: Programme Guidance*. https://www.sbcguidance.org/ Supports: doing this before designing. "Identify and engage with reference groups. Talk to community members to figure out whom they model their behaviour after and whose opinion matters most to them."
- World Bank (2015). *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0342-0 Supports: the disapproval question. Box 11.2: asking "would you worry about anyone disapproving?" can "help reveal the relevant network to which the social norm applies".
- Bicchieri, C., Lindemans, J. W., & Jiang, T. (2014). A structured approach to a diagnostic of collective practices. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 5, 1418. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01418 Supports: finding the group by asking. "Who exactly belongs to people's reference network is an empirical question." Full text.

The manual's ego-centric approach also asks respondents whom their contacts would name. This skill uses the plainer form described in the review by Perkins and colleagues below, in which people report only their own contacts.

Listing gatekeepers apart from influential people, the comparison with who the program reaches, and the "as reported" mark are this skill's own.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

None found: a study that checks a count of roles from short interviews against a full network census, or against who in fact changed. Searches covered systematic reviews of network studies in low- and middle-income countries, studies that identify reference groups for family planning, network targeting trials, the Social Norms Exploration Tool, and the UNICEF manual.

What depends on the place is whether people will name anyone, whether a wife can name a husband or an elder as an obstacle, and whether the people who matter live nearby at all.

## Replication

The finding that behavior goes with network ties, more than with the village as a unit, recurs across studies in low- and middle-income countries, most of them observational. The Honduran trial is experimental support for asking people to name others, and a warning that the most-named people are not the best route. The quick role count has pilots and no test of accuracy.

## What to check locally

1. Whether people will name anyone. In some places the honest answer is a role, "the elders", and the design should accept it.
2. Whether women name men freely, and younger people name older relatives; interview alone.
3. Whether "the health worker" or "the chief" is a polite answer. The question "if they changed their mind, would you?" is the check.
4. Whether people who live elsewhere belong on the map: a husband working away, a relative in another village, an employer.
5. Whether recording who blocks or punishes creates risk. If so, keep to roles and get the review before collecting.
