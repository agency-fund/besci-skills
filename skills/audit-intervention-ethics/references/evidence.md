# Evidence: Check an intervention is ethical before launch

## Sources

- OECD (2019). *Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit*. https://doi.org/10.1787/9ea76a8f-en Supports: steps 2 and 3, and four of the six checks. Read in part; the ethics guidance for its third stage, where strategies are chosen, was read in full. The toolkit draws two distinctions. An intervention is transparent unless citizens "cannot identify who is trying to influence them, by what means and for what purposes". It is avoidable when it makes conscious control possible or depends on it, and unavoidable when people cannot control the influence. Crossing the two gives four types, each with a guideline (numbers 7 to 10 in the toolkit's Table 2.7), which step 3 restates. The toolkit words each guideline as what is "usually regarded" as permissible; step 3 states them as conditions. Four considerations go with them: "Prioritise transparency", "Offer a way out", "Ensure the policy intervention serves the public interest", and ensure citizens are not "held responsible for consequences that they did not consciously select". The toolkit also says that public acceptance of an intervention does not make it ethically permissible, that being able to avoid an intervention in principle is not being able to avoid it in practice, and, in its guidance on scaling, that specific individuals and groups should not "pay a negative price for the average improvement".
- Hansen, P. G., & Jespersen, A. M. (2013). Nudge and the manipulation of choice. *European Journal of Risk Regulation*, 4(1), 3–28. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1867299X00002762 Supports: the four types, which the OECD toolkit adapts from this paper. Abstract only.
- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: the checks on whose interest and who bears the cost, and step 7. Read in full. The guide asks whether a target behavior "is ultimately in the best interest of the target group"; asks of each idea whether it has safety risks, negative side-effects, or "a negative effect on equity"; has the intervention reviewed by colleagues outside the project; and says whether an ethics board is needed depends on the organization's own rules, on whether the audience is vulnerable, and on whether the intervention could harm or distress. It advises using a board in the country where the work happens.
- Walton, G. M., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Wise interventions: Psychological remedies for social and personal problems. *Psychological Review*, 125(5), 617–655. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000115 Supports: the check on who bears the cost. The passage on ethical considerations read. Its fifth principle is that interventions can go wrong, so they should be tested at each step, and that the changes a reform puts first should be made explicit so that trade-offs between competing values come to light. Its example is a policing policy that may reduce crime and increase the harassment of racial minorities.
- Feeney, L., Kopper, S., & Sautmann, A. (2022). *Ethical conduct of randomized evaluations*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/ethical-conduct-randomized-evaluations Supports: the list of questions for an ethics board. Read in part. It is a guide to research ethics built on the three principles of the Belmont Report: respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. Those are the board's subject, and this skill does not rule on them.
- Lades, L. K., & Delaney, L. (2022). Nudge FORGOOD. *Behavioural Public Policy*, 6(1), 75–94. https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2019.53 A second published checklist, which the Behavioural Insights Team's guide points to. Abstract only. Its seven dimensions are fairness, openness, respect, goals, opinions, options, and delegation.
- Sunstein, C. R. (2015). The ethics of nudging. *Yale Journal on Regulation*, 32(2). https://www.yalejreg.com/print/the-ethics-of-nudging/ Abstract only. It asks whether a nudge promotes or undermines welfare, autonomy, and dignity.
- OECD (2022). *Good practice principles for ethical behavioural science in public policy*. OECD Public Governance Policy Papers. https://doi.org/10.1787/e19a9be9-en A later step-by-step guide from the same organization. Abstract only; nothing in the skill is taken from it.

Four rules are this skill's own and come from no source: writing the plain disclosure and asking whether the intervention survives it, the wording of the pressure check, rating each check for the least powerful person in the audience, and the rule that a verdict cannot be "go" above a failed check. On pressure, the OECD toolkit warns that people subject to a social-norm intervention "may feel stigmatised".

The procedure does not depend on the population. The answers do: who can decline and at what cost, who holds power over whom, and what counts as exposure are local facts, which is why the checks are rated for the least powerful person in the audience.

## Replication

Not applicable. The sources state principles and report no findings. None tests whether two reviewers given the same draft reach the same verdict, so treat a review as reasoning made visible, not as a clearance.

## What to check locally

1. Who gives consent for whom: the person, a household head, a parent, or a village assembly, and whether one person can realistically opt out where decisions are collective.
2. Whether being seen doing the behavior reads as recognition or as shame, and what happens to people seen not doing it.
3. What power the deliverer holds over the audience, which can turn an opt-out on paper into none in practice.
4. Which information is already public here and which is sensitive: health status, debt, pregnancy, legal status.
5. Whether a research ethics board must approve the intervention, the evaluation, or both, and how early it needs the draft.
