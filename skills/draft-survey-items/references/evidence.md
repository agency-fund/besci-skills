# Evidence: Write survey questions people can answer

## Sources

- Krosnick, J. A., & Presser, S. (2010). Question and questionnaire design. In P. V. Marsden & J. D. Wright (Eds.), *Handbook of Survey Research* (2nd ed., pp. 263–313). Emerald. https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf Supports: the wording rules in the design check. The chapter's list of conventional advice includes "Use simple, familiar words", "Ask about one thing at a time (avoid double-barreled questions)", "Avoid leading or loaded questions", and "Avoid questions with single or double negations". From its review of experiments: "reliability is higher when all points are labeled with words than when only some are"; yea-saying (the chapter's term is acquiescence) averages "about 10%" across the methods it reviews and is "most common among respondents who have lower social status" and "less formal education"; and "it seems best to avoid agree/disagree, true/false, and yes/no formats altogether and instead ask questions using rating scales that explicitly display the evaluative dimension". It also says reversed items do not solve yea-saying, because writing them usually needs negations, which are "cognitively burdensome and error-laden". On length, "7-point scales are probably optimal in many instances", with the caution that most of that research "involves visual administration". Read in full.
- Kopper, S., & Parry, K. (2023). *Survey design*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/survey-design Supports: naming the time period ("Each question should be SMART": specific, measurable, accessible, relevant, time-bound); reusing tested questions ("you should not start from scratch but instead start by reviewing existing, well-tested surveys"); ordering factual questions before attitudinal ones; not assuming "that lists of response options used elsewhere will be exhaustive in your context"; and the range of methods for sensitive questions, of which indirect techniques are one. Read in full.
- Gyani, A., Tindall, K., & Gallagher, R. (2022). *Explore Field Guide*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Explore-Field-Guide.pdf Supports: the plain-words, neutral-words, and time-period rules. Its checklist "How to write good survey questions" includes "Use simple language", "Use neutral language", "Be specific", "Use a relevant reference period", and "Allow for disagreement: People often agree just to avoid seeming disagreeable". Survey section read.
- Bjärkefur, K., Cardoso de Andrade, L., Daniels, B., & Jones, M. R. (2021). *Development Research in Practice: The DIME Analytics Data Handbook*. World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1694-9 Supports: starting from the list of outcomes to measure, and reuse: "using questions from reputable survey instruments that have already been fielded is recommended rather than creating questions from scratch". Chapter 4 read.
- Groves, R. M., Fowler, F. J., Couper, M. P., Lepkowski, J. M., Singer, E., & Tourangeau, R. (2009). *Survey Methodology* (2nd ed.). Wiley. A standard textbook that covers the development of questionnaires and the evaluation of questions. Cited for the topic only; the text was not read.
- Krosnick, J. A. (1991). Response strategies for coping with the cognitive demands of attitude measures in surveys. *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 5(3), 213–236. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2350050305 Supports: the mechanism. When answering well takes effort, some respondents give a merely satisfactory answer, for example "agreeing with an assertion made by a question" or choosing the first reasonable option. Abstract only.
- Laajaj, R., & Macours, K. (2021). Measuring skills in developing countries. *Journal of Human Resources*, 56(4), 1254–1295. https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.56.4.1018-9805R1 Supports: the yea-saying caution and the reversed-item exception. Read in the 2019 working-paper version.
- Chachamovich, E., Fleck, M. P., & Power, M. (2009). Literacy affected ability to adequately discriminate among categories in multipoint Likert scales. *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 62(1), 37–46. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2008.03.002 Supports: three options for respondents who do not read. Abstract only.
- Scott, K., Gharai, D., Sharma, M., Choudhury, N., Mishra, B., Chamberlain, S., & LeFevre, A. (2019). Yes, no, maybe so: the importance of cognitive interviewing to enhance structured surveys on respectful maternity care in northern India. *Health Policy and Planning*. https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czz141 Supports: short answer scales, and reports of what happened in place of hypothetical questions. Abstract only. The same team's later account of this work (Scott, K., Ummer, O., & LeFevre, A. E. (2021), *Health Policy and Planning*, 36(6), 982–995, https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czab048, read in full) states that "Likert scales with more than three response options were incomprehensible to most rural Indian women we interviewed".
- Schaeffer, N. C. (1991). Hardly ever or constantly? Group comparisons using vague quantifiers. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 55(3), 395. https://doi.org/10.1086/269270 Supports: asking for a count where one exists. In a 1975 national household sample (the abstract does not name the country), frequency phrases stood for higher absolute frequencies among white, better educated, and younger respondents. Abstract only.
- Swain, S. D., Weathers, D., & Niedrich, R. W. (2008). Assessing three sources of misresponse to reversed Likert items. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 45(1), 116–131. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.45.1.116 Supports: writing no reversed questions. Across four experiments, about 20% of respondents answered a reversed item on the same side of the scale as its unreversed partner, which the authors trace to the difficulty of checking a reversed statement against one's own view, not to inattention or yea-saying. Abstract only.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: writing the intended reading beside each question; asking for a count out of ten to measure a belief about other people; and leaving a stigmatized or illegal behavior unwritten, where the J-PAL guide lists rewording as one of several strategies.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Dykema, Schaeffer, Garbarski, Assad & Blixt (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sapharm.2021.06.014) | Review of experiments; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Experiments comparing agree/disagree statements with direct ("item-specific") questions "yield some mixed results", but more of them find direct questions give more valid and reliable answers and agree/disagree statements more yea-saying |
| [Saris, Revilla, Krosnick & Shaeffer (2010)](https://doi.org/10.18148/srm/2010.v4i1.2682) | Randomized experiments inside representative sample surveys; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Answers to agree/disagree questions "had much lower quality" than answers to comparable direct questions |
| [Lelkes & Weiss (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053168015604173) | Comparison within two waves of a national election study; abstract only | United States | Direct questions were as reliable and valid as agree/disagree ones, no better, including among the respondents most likely to agree with anything |
| [Laajaj & Macours (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.56.4.1018-9805R1) | Survey experiment with repeated measurement, about 900 farmers; working-paper version read | Kenya, repeated with 804 farmers in Colombia | On agree/disagree personality and attitude scales, the yea-saying score "is twice as large for somebody with no education compared to somebody with 10 years of education". In piloting, yea-saying "was more likely when respondents did not fully understand a question". Reversed items were sometimes harder to understand, but the authors keep them, balanced with the others, in order to measure and correct yea-saying. Yea-saying in Colombia was "of the same order of magnitude" |
| [Chachamovich, Fleck & Power (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2008.03.002) | Psychometric comparison of 141 non-readers with 907 graduates; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract; the first author is based in Brazil | On a five-point scale, non-readers "exhibit a response pattern indicative that only three categories are in fact considered". Reducing the number of options improved the fit for non-readers only |
| [Scott et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czz141) | Cognitive interviews with 21 rural women on 88 questions; abstract only | India | "Likert scale response options were generally incomprehensible and questions involving hypothetical scenarios could be interpreted in unexpected ways" |
| [Harzing (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1470595806066332) | Cross-national survey; abstract only | 26 countries, not listed in the abstract | Yea-saying and the use of extreme and middle options differ between countries |
| [Johnson, Kulesa, Cho & Shavitt (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022104272905) | About 18,000 employee questionnaires; abstract only | 19 countries, including Brazil, India, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, and Turkey | Yea-saying and extreme answers vary with country-level cultural measures |

The scan found no experiment outside the United States and Europe that compares agree/disagree statements with direct questions, no study from a low- or middle-income country of how respondents read frequency words such as "often", and no field test of three answer options against longer scales beyond the two studies above. It searched for meta-analyses of agree/disagree against item-specific questions, for yea-saying experiments in sub-Saharan Africa and India, for Likert scales with low-literacy respondents in low- and middle-income countries, for reversed items with low-literacy respondents, and for cross-cultural studies of vague frequency words.

What depends on local conditions is how hard the answering task is for the respondent: schooling, familiarity with surveys, and how much a respondent defers to the interviewer all raise yea-saying and shrink the number of options people can use.

## Replication

Krosnick and Presser call the evidence for yea-saying on agree/disagree statements "voluminous", and it is stronger where schooling is lower, both in the studies they review and in Kenya. The remedy is less settled: most experiments favor direct questions, a comparison within the American National Election Study found no gain, and the scan found no experiment that tested the remedy with respondents with little schooling. The evidence for short scales with non-readers rests on two studies.

## What to check locally

1. Whether respondents can tell the answer options apart. Ask five people to say in their own words the difference between two neighboring options.
2. Whether a local event or season bounds memory better than a number of days.
3. Whether tested questions on the same constructs exist in national surveys run in the same language.
4. Whether a construct that is harmless elsewhere is sensitive here: savings amounts, who decides in the household.
5. In the first days of fieldwork, the share of respondents giving the same answer to every rating question.
