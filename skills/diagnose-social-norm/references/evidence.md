# Evidence: Find out whether a practice is a social norm

## Sources

- Bicchieri, C. (2017). *Norms in the Wild: How to Diagnose, Measure, and Change Social Norms*. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780190622046.001.0001 Supports: the definitions and the order of the diagnosis. The chapter on measurement states that "measuring consensus on appropriate behavior is not sufficient to diagnose a social norm. Rather, we have to assess conditional preferences". Abstracts of the book and of that chapter only.
- Bicchieri, C., & Penn Social Norms Training and Consulting Group (2016). *Why Do People Do What They Do? A Social Norms Manual for Viet Nam, Indonesia and the Philippines*. UNICEF Office of Research – Innocenti. https://repository.upenn.edu/pennsong/12 Supports: the four questions and the classification. Its flow chart sorts a pattern of behavior into a custom, a moral norm, a descriptive norm, or a social norm, by whether people follow it "no matter what others do" and whether "normative expectations are also needed to motivate action". It warns that people resist counterfactual questions about themselves and their own networks, and offers vignettes as a way round. Sections 1, 3, and 5 read. This skill says "moral rule", the term in the article below, where the manual says "moral norm".
- Bicchieri, C., Lindemans, J. W., & Jiang, T. (2014). A structured approach to a diagnostic of collective practices. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 5, 1418. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01418 Supports: marking the call provisional, and reporting the gap. The questions "separate different kinds of motives", but "none of these diagnoses are final"; to learn whether expectations cause the practice "we want to manipulate expectations and see how individuals' choices change", with experiments or vignettes. It also warns that where a practice is criminalized or campaigned against, people give the "correct" answer. Full text.
- Cislaghi, B., & Heise, L. (2018). Theory and practice of social norms interventions: eight common pitfalls. *Globalization and Health*, 14, 83. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12992-018-0398-x Supports: listing what else holds the practice in place, and two of the failure modes. Among its eight lessons: norms and attitudes are different; harmful practices "are sustained by a matrix of factors"; the prevalence of a norm "is not necessarily a sign of its strength"; and "publicising the prevalence of a harmful practice can make things worse". Abstract only.
- World Bank (2015). *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0342-0 Supports: the wording of the questions. Box 11.2, on measurement techniques, has an entry "Identifying social norms" that gives "Out of 10 of your neighbors, how many exclusively breastfeed their children?" for what people expect others to do, and "would you worry about anyone disapproving?" for whose view counts.
- UNICEF (2022). *Social & Behaviour Change at UNICEF: Programme Guidance*. https://www.sbcguidance.org/ Supports: doing the diagnosis first. Its social norms tool says: "Not all behaviours are driven by social norms", and before designing an intervention a team should "establish whether norms are at play".

The required gap line, the "pending field answers" rule, and the rule against classifying from staff accounts are this skill's own.

None found: a study that tests whether the four questions classify a practice reliably, for instance two teams classifying the same practice, or the questions checked against a vignette experiment. Searches covered systematic reviews of social norm measurement in low- and middle-income countries, the Social Norms Exploration Tool, diagnostics of sanitation and child marriage, female genital cutting as a coordination norm, and meta-analyses of misperceived norms.

What depends on the place is who the reference group is, which sanctions are believable, and whether people will state an expectation to an outsider when the practice is illegal or an organization has campaigned against it.

## Replication

Contested, practice by practice. The Saudi study found the pattern the framework predicts: private support, a mistaken belief about others, and a change in behavior once the belief was corrected. The Sudan study tested a practice long treated as the standard example of a norm and did not find the pattern. Across field studies, correcting a mistaken belief about others changes the belief more dependably than the behavior. The question set has no published test of reliability or validity.

## What to check locally

1. Whether people will state expectations honestly in front of an outsider, in a group, or in front of the people they name. Ask the normative and consequence questions one person at a time.
2. Who people name when asked whose opinion matters; it is often not the program's audience.
3. Which sanctions are believable here (gossip, exclusion, loss of standing, violence) and who applies them.
4. Whether the true share of people who follow or favor the practice is known, before anyone says people misjudge it.
5. Whether the practice is illegal or harmful, which changes how the questions may be asked and who must review them first.
