# Evidence: Plan how you will measure success

## Sources

- Kettle, S., & Persian, R. (2022). *Target, Explore, Solution, Trial, Scale: An introduction to running simple behavioural insights projects*. The Behavioural Insights Team. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Handbook-How-to-run-simple-BI-projects.pdf Supports: step 2, from Box 1.4, "Choosing an outcome": focus "on a target behaviour as your primary outcome", because "fewer other factors will influence changes in it compared to the overall objective". Step 3, from "Find data to measure impact": the four data types in order ("Existing, routinely collected data"; "New measures added to routinely collected data or adaptations to data collection process"; "Survey or observational data"; "Proxy measures"), where existing data is "the ideal situation" and a proxy is "a measure that you expect to be highly correlated with your outcome of interest". Step 4: who holds the data, at what level it is available, what approvals are needed, when it is available, and in what format; and, for surveys, whether people "have an interest in reporting honestly". Chapter 4 allows "one or two primary outcomes". Read in full.
- Haynes, L., Service, O., Goldacre, B., & Torgerson, D. (2012). *Test, Learn, Adapt*. Cabinet Office. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7488c8e5274a7f9c586c23/TLA-1906126.pdf Supports: steps 2, 5, and 8, from step 2 of its nine ("Define the outcome that the policy is intended to influence"): "be specific about how and when the outcomes will be measured at the design stage of the trial, and to stick with these prespecified outcomes at the analysis stage"; "the way outcomes are measured for all the groups is exactly the same"; choose "an outcome that you really care about, or as close as you can get to it, rather than a procedural measure that is halfway there". Box 11 on surrogate outcomes supports the proxy rule: the case for one "is strongest where there is good evidence that it is a strong predictor of the ultimate outcome", and self-reported behavior change "can be a poor index of actual behavioural change" because of social desirability. Step 2 read in full.
- Kopper, S., & Parry, K. (2022). *Introduction to measurement and indicators*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/introduction-measurement-and-indicators Supports: step 4's checks on existing records, from its list for secondary data: whether the data exist and are accessible, whether they were "collected consistently across that date range", whether they cover the population and the outcomes of interest, and whether they are "reliable and unlikely to have been manipulated". Also the proxy test: a proxy must be able "to change in tandem with the construct"; and the rule behind "recorded the same way for everyone compared": "Bias that is correlated with the treatment is more serious than bias that is not."
- Duflo, E., Glennerster, R., & Kremer, M. (2007). Using randomization in development economics research: A toolkit. In *Handbook of Development Economics* (pp. 3895–3962). Elsevier. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1573-4471(07)04061-2 Supports: step 6. "As a rule of thumb for a policy intervention evaluation, this should be the smallest effect size that is large enough such that the intervention would be cost effective if it were to be scaled up." Read in the NBER Technical Working Paper 333 version (2006), sections 3 to 5.
- Gupta, S., & Kopper, S. (2021). *Power calculations*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/power-calculations Supports: step 6 and "A borrowed threshold". The minimum detectable effect "may be a practically relevant effect size such as the minimum improvement in outcomes required for the implementing partner to determine the program's benefits are worth the costs or to scale the program", and "published studies evaluating similar interventions will typically provide an optimistic estimate of a reasonable effect size."
- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: the replication note. "A sharp distinction between one primary outcome and several secondary outcomes is not necessarily appropriate, particularly where the programme theory identifies impacts across a range of domains." Full text; the passage on outcomes read.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: one primary outcome even where the sources allow two; a named person beside every feasibility answer; and the dated outcome statement, which is kept and superseded rather than edited.

The procedure does not depend on the population. The data do: whether routine records exist for everyone, whether they are kept the same way in every area, and whether the people who keep them are rewarded on the same number.

## Replication

Not applicable. None of the sources tests whether plans made this way lead to better decisions. They disagree on one point: the Behavioural Insights Team allows one or two primary outcomes, and the Medical Research Council's guidance says a sharp split between one primary outcome and the rest is not always appropriate.

## What to check locally

1. Whether the records chosen cover people the program did not reach, kept the same way.
2. Who in the data-holding organization can grant access, and how long approval takes.
3. Whether anyone who records the outcome is paid, rated, or promoted on it.
4. Who has authority to set the smallest change worth detecting, and whether they have done so in writing.
