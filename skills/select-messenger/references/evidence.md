# Evidence: Choose who delivers the message

## Sources

- Dolan, P., Hallsworth, M., Halpern, D., King, D., & Vlaev, I. (2010). *MINDSPACE: Influencing behaviour through public policy*. Institute for Government and Cabinet Office. https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/publications/MINDSPACE.pdf Supports: the properties rated in step 4. The report's section on the messenger, read in full, says "we are heavily influenced by who communicates information": people are affected by the messenger's perceived authority, formal or informal; by demographic and behavioral similarity between messenger and recipient; and by their feelings toward the messenger, which "may override traditional cues of authority", so that people who dislike government may discount a message seen to come from it.
- BenYishay, A., & Mobarak, A. M. (2019). Social learning and incentives for experimentation and communication. *The Review of Economic Studies*, 86(3), 976–1009. https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdy039 Supports: putting likeness first when people doubt that others like them do the behavior, and stating in the brief what the messenger receives. Abstract only.
- Banerjee, A., Chandrasekhar, A. G., Duflo, E., & Jackson, M. O. (2019). Using gossips to spread information: Theory and evidence from two randomized controlled trials. *The Review of Economic Studies*, 86(6), 2453–2490. https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdz008 Supports: the reach question in step 3, and putting reach first when people have not heard. Abstract only.
- Alsan, M., & Eichmeyer, S. (2024). Experimental evidence on the effectiveness of nonexperts for improving vaccine demand. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 16(1), 394–414. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20210393 Supports: rating expertise and trust separately. Abstract only.
- Pornpitakpan, C. (2004). The persuasiveness of source credibility: A critical review of five decades' evidence. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology*, 34(2), 243–281. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2004.tb02547.x Supports: the same separation. A credible source is usually more persuasive than a less credible one, and under certain conditions credibility is a liability. Abstract only.

Three parts of the method are this skill's own and have not been tested as a package: the rule in step 5 that matches the messenger to the kind of barrier, the trust question in step 3, and the check for power in step 6. MINDSPACE is the framework that names the messenger as a technique in its own right; the 2024 edition of the Behavioural Insights Team's *EAST* does not use the term.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Pornpitakpan (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2004.tb02547.x) | Narrative review of five decades of studies of source credibility; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | High-credibility sources are generally more persuasive, with conditions under which credibility is a liability. The outcomes are mostly persuasion, not behavior |
| [Medley, Kennedy, O'Reilly & Sweat (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1521/aeap.2009.21.3.181) | Systematic review and meta-analysis, 30 studies of peer education for HIV prevention; abstract only | Developing countries; not listed in the abstract | Peer education was associated with more HIV knowledge (odds ratio 2.28) and more condom use (odds ratio 1.92), with no significant effect on sexually transmitted infections. It compares peer education with none, not peers with other messengers |
| [BenYishay & Mobarak (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdy039) | Randomized field experiment with different kinds of communicator; abstract only | Malawi | Farmers were most convinced by communicators who shared a group identity with them or faced comparable farming conditions. Communicators' own adoption and effort responded to small performance incentives |
| [Banerjee, Chandrasekhar, Duflo & Jackson (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdz008) | Two randomized field experiments; abstract only | India | In 521 villages in Haryana, information about immunization camps was given either to people chosen at random or to people villagers named as good at passing information on. The number of children vaccinated each month was 22% higher where the named people received it. The named people were central in the village network, not only those with many friends or in powerful positions |
| [Banerjee et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA19739) | Randomized trial of 75 combinations of reminders, incentives, and local ambassadors; abstract only | India (Haryana) | The combination with the largest effect (incentives, ambassadors who are information hubs, and reminders) raised the number of immunizations by 44%. The most cost-effective (information-hub ambassadors with text reminders, no incentives) raised immunizations per dollar by 9.1%. These are results for bundles, not for the messenger alone |
| [Alatas, Chandrasekhar, Mobius, Olken & Paladines (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/uead102) | Randomized experiment on Twitter; abstract only | Indonesia | Messages promoting vaccination were 72% more likely to be passed on or liked when they came from a celebrity than similar messages without a celebrity's name. Citing an outside medical source cut retweets by 27% |
| [Beaman, BenYishay, Magruder & Mobarak (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20200295) | Randomized field experiment in 200 villages; abstract only | Malawi | Giving information about a new farming method to farmers central in the village network mattered for whether it spread. Many farmers needed to learn from several people before adopting |
| [Airoldi & Christakis (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adi5147) | Randomized trial in 176 villages, 24,702 people; abstract only | Honduras | Choosing the households that received health education by asking villagers to name friends reduced the number of households needed to reach a given level of village-wide uptake. Knowledge spread more readily than behavior |
| [Alsan & Eichmeyer (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20210393) | Randomized experiment varying the sender of a vaccine message; abstract only | United States | Among men without a college education, lay people like the audience were the most effective senders, most of all for those least willing to be vaccinated. The authors describe a trade-off between an expert's qualifications and social distance from the audience |

No systematic review was found that compares kinds of messenger by their effect on behavior. Outside wealthy Western countries the field trials are mostly about how far information spreads from the people it is first given to; the Malawi communicator trial is the one that compares who is believed. What depends on the place is which people others turn to, whether those people are the same for news and for advice, and how much standing an official or an expert carries on the topic.

## Replication

Mixed. That the messenger matters has support from several countries. Which messenger does best is not settled: peers who shared the audience's conditions in Malawi, people named as good at spreading news in India, celebrities in Indonesia, and lay people over experts in one United States sample. These studies measured different things, and none tested a rule for choosing among kinds of messenger. The India result has two trials behind it, both by the same research group.

## What to check locally

1. Whether "whom should we tell so that everyone hears?" and "whose advice would you act on?" name the same people.
2. Whether the people named are willing, what they expect in return, and how many other programs already use them.
3. Whether the current sender is in fact heard from; a monthly visit that reaches a third of households is not reach.
4. Whether any candidate controls wages, credit, land, permits, grades, or access to a service.
5. Whether being approached by this messenger about this topic tells others something private about the person approached.
