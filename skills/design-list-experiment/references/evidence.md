# Evidence: Measure a sensitive behavior without asking directly

## Sources

- Blair, G., Coppock, A., & Moor, M. (2020). When to worry about sensitivity bias: A social reference theory and evidence from 30 years of list experiments. *American Political Science Review*, 114(4), 1297–1315. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055420000374 Supports: the go or no-go step. "Under typical conditions, list experiments are approximately 14 times noisier than direct questions, which means that either the sample size or the amount of bias needs to be large in order to justify a list experiment." With 2,000 respondents a typical list estimate has a standard error of about 4 percentage points, against about 1 for a direct question. "For a study with 1,000 subjects, the bias must be greater than 5.5 points to prefer a list experiment; at 2,000, the bias must be greater than 4 points." To show that a list and a direct question differ, "at 1,000 subjects, the bias would need to exceed 16 percentage points in order to reach 80% power". To tell two groups apart, "differences must exceed 20 percentage points before a 2,000 unit study has 80% power to detect them". For 98 of the 264 experiments, a direct question would likely have come closer to the truth. The authors add that "ensuring the safety of participants and research staff may be well worth the loss of precision", and that changes which separate the answer from the respondent for a time, such as a secret ballot or a self-completed questionnaire, "can reduce bias without increasing variance". A double list, in which every respondent gets the sensitive statement in one of two lists, has about half the variance. Full text.
- Blair, G., & Imai, K. (2012). Statistical analysis of list experiments. *Political Analysis*, 20(1), 47–77. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr048 Supports: the control statements and the checks. The method rests on two assumptions: "the inclusion of a sensitive item has no effect on respondents' answers to control items" (no design effect), and "respondents give truthful answers for the sensitive item" (no liars). The paper proposes a statistical test of the first. On the second: a ceiling effect arises because "privacy is not protected for those respondents in the treatment group whose true preferences are affirmative for all the sensitive and control items", and a floor effect when the control statements are expected to draw many negative answers. "Ideally, control items should be chosen such that fewer extreme responses are observed in the control group." Sections 1 to 3 read in part.
- Blair, G., Chou, W., & Imai, K. *list: Statistical Methods for the Item Count Technique and List Experiment*. R package. https://cran.r-project.org/package=list Supports: the software named in step 7. Its description includes a test for certain failures of a list experiment.
- Kramon, E., & Weghorst, K. (2019). (Mis)measuring sensitive attitudes with the list experiment. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 83(S1), 236–263. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfz009 Supports: private counting, and the first failure mode. In Kenya, "over 40 percent of respondents provide inconsistent responses between list experiment and direct question formats" on harmless lists about food and daily activities, with errors "concentrated among less numerate and less educated respondents". Letting respondents tally in private, and cartoon picture aids, "greatly enhance list experiment performance". Abstract only.
- Glynn, A. N. (2013). What can we learn with statistical truth serum? *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 77(S1), 159–172. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfs070 Supports: the advice on control statements, and the inputs a sample-size calculation needs. The paper "presents design principles for the standard list experiment (and the double list experiment) for the reduction of bias and variance as well as providing sample-size formulas". Abstract only. The advice to include control statements that rarely go together is reported by Blair, Coppock, and Moor (2020), who credit it to Miller's 1984 dissertation and say this paper develops it.
- Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL). *Survey design*. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/survey-design Supports: trying privacy first. Its section "Sensitive questions" lists, before any indirect method, a private setting and "online surveys or self-administered modules". It then describes list randomization: "A group is divided into two subgroups and asked how many statements from a list are true."
- World Bank (2015). *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0342-0 Supports: the task as a named technique. Box 11.2 calls list experiments a "method for measuring the share of a population that engages in a taboo behavior or holds an opinion that may not be freely admitted", in which "the lists differ solely in the presence of the sensitive item or topic".
- Groves, R. M., Fowler, F. J., Couper, M. P., Lepkowski, J. M., Singer, E., & Tourangeau, R. (2009). *Survey Methodology* (2nd ed.). Wiley. Supports: the topic only. Section 7.4.2 is "Sensitive Questions About Behavior". Table of contents only.

The practice list, the reporting rule fixed before fielding, and the rule that a list estimate below the direct one counts as a failure are this skill's own.

The 19 vote-buying experiments are from developing countries | Direct questions are typically off by less than 10 percentage points. | [Li & Van den Noortgate (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0049124119882468) | Meta-analysis of 246 effect sizes from 54 studies; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | The sensitive item was reported 8.9 percentage points more often by list than by direct question. The gain varied with whether the setting was collectivist or individualist and with the number of control items |
| [Gonzalez-Ocantos, de Jonge, Meléndez, Osorio & Nickerson (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2011.00540.x) | List experiment in a national survey; abstract only | Nicaragua | 24% of registered voters were offered a gift or favor for their vote by the list estimate; 2% said so when asked directly |
| [Lépine, Treibich, Ndour, Gueye & Vickerman (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czz155) | List experiment with 786 female sex workers in 2015 and 2017; abstract only | Senegal | Condom use with the last client was 80% and 78% by list, against 97% in face-to-face questions |
| [Cullen (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/lhac022) | Women randomly assigned to a list or to direct questions; abstract only | Nigeria | Rates of partner violence were "up to 35 percent greater" by list |
| [Agüero & Frisancho (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1086/714008) | List experiments against standard direct questions; abstract only | Peru | "No significant differences" in reported physical or sexual violence, overall and in 16 subgroups |
| [Haber and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-018-0507-9) | Randomized test with 483 people whose HIV status and testing history were known; abstract only | South Africa | The list did worse than direct questions: it put the HIV-negative share at 56%, direct questions at 47%, and the true figure was 32%. The authors point to "difficulties in implementation or comprehension" |
| [Kramon & Weghorst (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfz009) | List experiments with variations in how respondents counted; abstract only | Kenya | The standard list showed less support for political violence than the direct question, read by the authors as a breakdown. Private tallying and picture aids improved it |
| [Chuang, Dupas, Huillery & Seban (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2020.102582) | Consistency tests built into two surveys on sexual and reproductive health; abstract only | Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire | "Non-compliance with instructions among surveyed individuals appears high and not easily characterizable" |
| [Peterman, Palermo, Handa & Seidenfeld (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1002/hec.3588) | List experiment inside the evaluation of a cash transfer; abstract only | Zambia | The method "functioned as planned" and put physical partner violence in the past year at about 15%; the authors call for more work on the method |
| [Karlan & Zinman (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2011.08.006) | List experiment on how borrowers used loans; abstract only | Peru, Philippines | Asking directly "under-reports the non-enterprise uses of loan proceeds" |

The one test found against a known answer outside these countries is from the United States: [Rosenfeld, Imai & Shapiro (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12205) compared survey answers with the official result of a 2011 referendum in Mississippi, and the list came closer to it than the direct question did.

What depends on the place is whether respondents can keep a count of statements read aloud, which fell with schooling and numeracy in Kenya; who the respondent fears will learn the answer; and whether statements that are harmless in one place are sensitive in another.

## Replication

Mixed. Three meta-analyses agree that lists move answers in the expected direction on average and that results vary widely from study to study. Field studies in low- and middle-income countries split: the list found more of the hidden behavior in Nicaragua, Senegal, Nigeria, Peru (loan use), and the Philippines, no difference in Peru (violence), and a wrong answer where the truth was known in South Africa. Where a list returns less of a behavior than the direct question, as in Kenya, the likelier reading is that respondents could not do the task. The meta-analysis authors warn that failed list experiments are less likely to be published.

## What to check locally

1. Whether respondents can hear four or five statements and report a count. Test with a few respondents, with and without a private way to tally.
2. Whether each control statement is harmless here and true for roughly the share expected.
3. Who respondents fear will learn their answer: the interviewer, someone in the room, the clinic, the authorities. If it is the interviewer or a bystander, self-completion may do the job at no cost in precision.
4. How large under-reporting plausibly is here, from records or other data, since the figure from other settings is a weak guide.
5. Whether a figure for a group, village, or district could be used against that group.
