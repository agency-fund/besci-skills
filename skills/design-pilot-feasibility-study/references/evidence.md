# Evidence: Design a pilot that tests feasibility, not effect

## Sources

The task is a named step in these guides:

- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., Craig, P., Baird, J., Blazeby, J. M., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: the five headings and the progression criteria. "A feasibility study should be designed to assess predefined progression criteria that relate to the evaluation design (eg, reducing uncertainty around recruitment, data collection, retention, outcomes, and analysis) or the intervention itself (eg, around optimal content and delivery, acceptability, adherence, likelihood of cost effectiveness, or capacity of providers to deliver the intervention)." Read in full.
- OECD (2019). *Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit*. https://doi.org/10.1787/9ea76a8f-en Supports: the task, and the replication note. Step 7 of its experiment stage, "Conduct a pilot-experiment", examines "whether institutional, technical and systemic aspects work out as expected", "challenges to time schedules", "potential indicators of what effect size of the intervention to expect", "the feasibility of the planned data analysis", and "whether revisions to the prototype and the protocol are needed". The step before it asks teams to "define potential 'ABORT' conditions". Those steps read.
- UNICEF (2022). *Social & Behaviour Change at UNICEF: Programme Guidance*. https://www.sbcguidance.org/ Supports: the task. "Test the impact of your interventions in stages. Start with a feasibility test or pilot to understand the feasibility, acceptability and potential impact of designs." Read in part, from an archived copy.
- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2011). *Beginning with the end in mind*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241502320 Supports: choosing typical sites and routine conditions. "Pilot projects tend to be implemented with a level of input and support that subsequently cannot be sustained when innovations are taken to scale." Recommendation 6 is to test the innovation "in the variety of sociocultural and institutional settings where it will be scaled-up"; recommendation 7 is to test it "under the routine operating conditions and existing resource constraints", to "keep externally provided nonreplicable inputs to a minimum", and, if necessary, to test first for "proof of concept" and then for "proof of implementation". Read in full.

The method:

- Eldridge, S. M., Chan, C. L., Campbell, M. J., Bond, C. M., Hopewell, S., Thabane, L., & Lancaster, G. A. (2016). CONSORT 2010 statement: extension to randomised pilot and feasibility trials. *BMJ*, 355, i5239. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i5239 Supports: the go, amend, and stop lines, the sizing, and the refusal to size from the pilot's effect. "A decision process about how to proceed needs to be built into the design of the pilot trial. This might involve formal progression criteria to decide whether to proceed, to proceed with amendments, or not to proceed", with measures "below a lower threshold" meaning not feasible, "above a higher threshold" feasible, and between the two feasible "if appropriate changes can be made". "The number of participants in a pilot study should be based on the feasibility objectives and some rationale should be given." "Formal hypothesis testing for effectiveness (or efficacy) is not recommended." The authors "caution against any reliance on estimates of effect size from pilot trials", while outcome data "can be legitimately used for other purposes, such as estimating inputs for sample size for the future definitive RCT". Methods "can be qualitative or quantitative". Read in full.
- Eldridge, S. M., Lancaster, G. A., Campbell, M. J., Thabane, L., Hopewell, S., Coleman, C. L., & Bond, C. M. (2016). Defining feasibility and pilot studies in preparation for randomised controlled trials: Development of a conceptual framework. *PLOS ONE*, 11(3), e0150205. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0150205 Supports: the definition. "A feasibility study asks whether something can be done, should we proceed with it, and if so, how. A pilot study asks the same questions but also has a specific design feature: in a pilot study a future study, or part of a future study, is conducted on a smaller scale." Abstract only.
- Leon, A. C., Davis, L. L., & Kraemer, H. C. (2011). The role and interpretation of pilot studies in clinical research. *Journal of Psychiatric Research*, 45(5), 626–629. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2010.10.008 Supports: the same refusal. A pilot "can be used to evaluate the feasibility of recruitment, randomization, retention, assessment procedures, new methods, and implementation"; it "is not a hypothesis testing study"; and it "does not provide a meaningful effect size estimate for planning subsequent studies due to the imprecision inherent in data from small samples". "Feasibility results do not necessarily generalize beyond the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the pilot design." Abstract only.
- Kraemer, H. C., Mintz, J., Noda, A., Tinklenberg, J., & Yesavage, J. A. (2006). Caution regarding the use of pilot studies to guide power calculations for study proposals. *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 63(5), 484. https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.63.5.484 Supports: the same. When pilots are used to guide power calculations, "the most likely outcomes are that (1) studies worth performing are aborted and (2) studies that are not aborted are underpowered." Abstract only.
- Avery, K. N. L., Williamson, P. R., Gamble, C., O'Connell Francischetto, E., Metcalfe, C., Davidson, P., Williams, H., & Blazeby, J. M. (2017). Informing efficient randomised controlled trials: exploration of challenges in developing progression criteria for internal pilot studies. *BMJ Open*, 7(2), e013537. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-013537 Supports: the headings. Progression criteria vary widely, but "3 common issues predominate: trial recruitment, protocol adherence and outcome data", and reporting of the decision to stop, amend, or proceed "is uncommon". Abstract only.
- Bowen, D. J., Kreuter, M., Spring, B., Cofta-Woerpel, L., Linnan, L., Weiner, D., Bakken, S., Kaplan, C. P., Squiers, L., Fabrizio, C., & Fernandez, M. (2009). How we design feasibility studies. *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 36(5), 452–457. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2009.02.002 Supports: the acceptability and delivery questions. The paper proposes eight areas of focus for feasibility studies: acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, adaptation, integration, expansion, and limited-efficacy testing. Its last area means testing an intervention "in a limited way", which this skill leaves out. Read in part.

Two rules are this skill's own and come from no source: sizing by the 95% margin on the most important rate, compared with the gap between the go and stop lines; and the requirement that every amend band name the change it triggers.

The procedure does not depend on the population. The rates do: recruitment, retention, and delivery move with season, site, and the people who must agree before anyone is approached, so the pilot's sites and season decide what its rates can say.

## Replication

Not applicable; this is method guidance. One point is contested. OECD lists "potential indicators of what effect size of the intervention to expect" among what a pilot examines, and UNICEF names "potential impact"; the CONSORT extension and Leon et al. warn against relying on a pilot's effect estimate. This skill follows the trial guidance and lets the pilot measure other inputs to the main trial's sample size instead. The reviews above show that most pilots state no progression criteria at all.

## What to check locally

1. How recruitment actually happens: gatekeepers, community entry, consent norms, and who must agree before anyone is approached.
2. Whether the data system the main study assumes, such as registers, pill counts, or transaction logs, exists at the pilot sites in the same form.
3. Whether the pilot season matches the main study's: planting, school terms, rains, migration.
4. Whether the pilot sites are typical of the main study's, or the most cooperative ones.
5. What local ethics review requires for a pilot that involves medication, children, or vulnerable participants, including a stopping rule for harm.
