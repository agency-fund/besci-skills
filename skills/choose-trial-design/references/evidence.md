# Evidence: Pick how to randomize the test

## Sources

- Haynes, L., Service, O., Goldacre, B., & Torgerson, D. (2012). *Test, Learn, Adapt*. Cabinet Office. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7488c8e5274a7f9c586c23/TLA-1906126.pdf Supports: the multi-arm row, from step 1 ("Identify two or more policy interventions to compare (e.g. old vs new policy; different variations of a policy)"); the unit, from step 3, which may be "individuals, institutions (e.g. schools), or geographical areas", where groups mean "a larger sample of individuals is required, and special methods of analysis are also needed", and where whole classes were once randomized "to avoid resentful demoralisation"; the stepped-wedge row, from Box 10, where a staggered roll-out with "a willingness to randomly decide which area goes first" becomes "a 'stepped-wedge' design trial"; and step 6 of the skill, from step 5, because "people who have a vested interest in a study may try to allocate people in a nonrandom manner, albeit unconsciously". Steps 1 to 5 read.
- Kopper, S., & Sautmann, A. (2023). *Randomization*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization Supports: the cluster row: "At a minimum, the unit of randomization should be the unit at which the treatment will be delivered, and ideally at a unit that minimizes spillovers"; "moving up in level of randomization decreases the study's effective sample size". Step 4: stratification variables "should thus be ones you expect to be correlated with the outcome of interest, or those for which you are interested in differential or heterogeneous treatment effects". Step 5: a draw in software from a list is replicable, while a public lottery "may hence be preferable in instances where it is desirable or necessary to actually show participants that their treatment assignment is random" but "is not replicable". The specialist note on spread as the question: designs that vary "the share of observational units in the cluster that receive the treatment". Read in full.
- Duflo, E., Glennerster, R., & Kremer, M. (2007). Using randomization in development economics research: A toolkit. In *Handbook of Development Economics* (pp. 3895–3962). Elsevier. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1573-4471(07)04061-2 Supports: the stepped-wedge row and "A phase-in that measures nothing": randomizing the order "can allow evaluation of program effects in contexts where it is not acceptable for some groups or individuals to receive no support"; "the time between phases should be sufficient to encompass any treatment lag"; and phase-in fails "when the comparison group is affected by the expectation of future treatment". The encouragement row: designs for a program "available in the entire study area but whose take up is not universal", whose power falls with the difference in take-up between groups. The factorial row and "A factorial read for interactions": with two cross-cut interventions there are four groups, and testing whether one works differently with the other needs a sample "sufficient to allow her to statistically distinguish A versus A and B, as well as B versus A and B". The multi-arm row: comparing two similar treatments "may require a larger sample size than the evaluation of either treatment separately would". "An assignment nobody follows": "It is much easier for a research team to ensure that villages are being treated according to an initial random assignment than to monitor individuals." Read in the NBER Technical Working Paper 333 version (2006), sections 3 to 5.
- Hemming, K., Haines, T. P., Chilton, P. J., Girling, A. J., & Lilford, R. J. (2015). The stepped wedge cluster randomised trial: rationale, design, analysis, and reporting. *BMJ*, 350, h391. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.h391 Supports: the stepped-wedge row and the specialist note on it. The design "can reconcile the need for robust evaluations with political or logistical constraints"; "the effect of the intervention might be confounded with any underlying temporal trend"; "Sample size calculations and analysis must make allowance for both the clustered nature of the design and the confounding effect of time"; and when the intra-cluster correlation is small "parallel studies tend to deliver better statistical performance than a stepped wedge trial", while with larger correlations or large clusters the stepped wedge "will be more powerful". Abstract only.
- Gupta, S., & Kopper, S. (2021). *Power calculations*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/power-calculations Supports: the multi-arm row ("Ensure that the study is powered for the smallest MDE among the comparisons that will be made (though this can significantly increase costs if comparing two similar treatments)") and the encouragement row as an option where withholding is a concern, "where the entire eligible population has access to the program, but only the treatment group receives encouragements to take it up".
- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: the rule for when no randomized design fits: "Non-randomised designs and modelling approaches might work best if a randomised design is not practical, for example, in natural experiments or systems evaluations." Full text; the passage on designs read.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: every unstated constraint is marked "unknown"; a runner-up is always named with the condition that would make it the choice; and the output names the person who agreed to any delay or withholding.

The designs do not depend on the population. The constraints do: how far a message, a price, or a disease travels between people; how many administrative units exist; and who has the authority to hold a rollout to a random order.

## Replication

Not applicable: these are design methods, not interventions. Their limits are stated in the sources: a stepped-wedge estimate can be confounded with time; a phase-in can be too fast for the effect to show; an encouragement design loses power as the difference in take-up shrinks; and a factorial design sized for each component cannot say much about how they combine.

## What to check locally

1. Whether people assigned to different arms really share the channel the change travels through, from staff who work in the field.
2. The rollout calendar and how many units can start each period.
3. Who has authority to delay or withhold, and whether they will hold to the assignment once drawn.
4. How many units exist at each level; a small count of districts rules out designs that look fine on paper.
