## What it does

Drafts a values-affirmation exercise, a short writing task about a personally important value, for one audience facing one moment of judgment.

**What this adds:** stops if the barrier is practical or the setting offers nothing to gain; builds the value list for this audience without the domain under threat, and labels an unconfirmed list a guess; keeps the tested form of a list, a choice, and writing about the chosen value; and fixes delivery as private, before the moment, and never announced as help.

The exercise is Cohen and Sherman's self-affirmation, one of Walton and Wilson's wise interventions: short exercises that change how people interpret their situation.

## When to use it

### Use it when
- People feel judged at a known moment (about money, school results, a health condition), and defensiveness keeps them from what the program offers
- Someone asks for "something to help people feel less judged about X before we ask them to do Y"
- A team is about to copy a published US value list

### Do not use it when
- The barrier is cost, access, or a process that exposes people (a visible queue, a public list); use `select-intervention-levers`
- Nobody has established what people feel judged about; use `identify-wise-intervention-target`

## Before you start

Ask these before producing the exercise, unless already answered.

1. **Required.** Who the people are and what they feel judged about, in their words.
2. **Required.** The moment the judgment is felt, and how the exercise can be given before it: written or spoken, in private or not.
3. **Required.** What people gain once they engage, and any practical barrier that keeps them away.
4. Optional. Which values matter here, from someone of the group.

If 1 or 2 is missing, ask and stop. If 3 is unknown, mark the check "not verified" and the exercise "not for use". If 4 is missing, adapt the list by best guess and label it "unconfirmed".

## What it draws on

- Cohen, G. L., & Sherman, D. K. (2014). The psychology of change: Self-affirmation and social psychological intervention. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 65, 333–371. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137 Supports: the mechanism. Writing about core values widens people's view of themselves, so a threat says less about their worth.
- Wu, Z., Spreckelsen, T. F., & Cohen, G. L. (2021). A meta-analysis of the effect of values affirmation on academic achievement. *Journal of Social Issues*, 77(3), 702–750. https://doi.org/10.1111/josi.12415 Supports: steps 2 to 6. Across 58 classroom studies, students under threat gained slightly (g = 0.15) and others not at all.
- Hanselman, P., Rozek, C. S., Grigg, J., & Borman, G. D. (2017). New evidence on self-affirmation effects and theorized sources of heterogeneity from large-scale replications. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 109(3), 405–424. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000141 Supports: step 7. A replication where the exercise had worked before found no effect.

**Where the evidence comes from:** All but 9 of the 58 classroom studies were run in the United States. Elsewhere the exercise raised test scores for rural-registered students in one school in China, had no effect on HIV testing among sex workers in Senegal, and, in a bundle of three exercises, changed no target outcome in Kenya. Check locally which values people hold and whether privacy is possible.

**Replication status:** Mixed. A meta-analysis finds a small average benefit for people under threat; a large school replication found none.

## How to do it

1. **Restate the people, the threat, and the moment**, close to their words.
2. **Check the setting, as a gate.** If cost, access, or exposure by design keeps people away, say the exercise will not change that, and stop. Write what people gain once they engage: in schools the exercise helped where resources to improve were present, not where they were scarce.
3. **Build the value list:** eight to twelve values that matter to this group, including values held with family or community. Do not copy a US list. Leave out the domain under threat (money, if the shame is about money) and any value that could carry the stigma, such as faith where the stigma is moral. Without input from someone of the group, label the list "unconfirmed".
4. **Write the selection prompt:** the value most important to you, not the one you think you should choose.
5. **Write the two-part prompt:** why this value matters to you, then a specific time it mattered. Add nothing; studies that changed the tested materials found smaller effects. Weak readers tell it to a facilitator in private.
6. **Write the delivery notes:** before the moment, since an affirmation that follows a defensive reaction comes too late; in private, ungraded, unread by staff; as an ordinary activity, never announced as help, which marks people as needing it; about 15 minutes; repeated only before a later stressful moment.
7. **Write the two cautions:** what the exercise does not fix, and what trials found, with no effect size promised.

See `references/worked-example.md` for a complete run.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Used in place of a fix.** It costs nothing, so it gets offered where the fix is a private waiting room or a fee waiver.
- **Positive self-talk.** "Affirmation" is often taken to mean repeating "I am capable", a different exercise that nothing here supports.
- **Made public.** Sharing values aloud, or staff collecting the writing, turns a private act into a performance before the people who judge.
- **One pilot read as the verdict.** Results differed between two cohorts in the same schools; one null or one gain settles nothing.

## When to bring in a specialist

Tell the user to consult a clinical psychologist, a counselor, or a safeguarding lead when:
- The stigma is clinical or carries acute risk (mental illness, HIV status, violence): the writing can surface distress, so clinical review and a referral path come first
- Participants are minors: consent and what happens to the writing need a child-protection review
- The group has lived through recent conflict or disaster; "a specific time" can land on a traumatic memory
- A pilot shows a negative effect

Bring them: the value list, the prompts, and the delivery notes.
