# Worked example: first-generation college students in a financial-literacy program

A full run of the skill on a realistic case, showing the output it should produce. The case is illustrative, not a real program.

## The request

"We run a financial-literacy program for first-generation college students. Many avoid asking questions in workshops or one-on-one advising because they feel judged or embarrassed talking about money: 'I should already know how credit scores work', 'I don't want to say I can't afford what everyone else takes for granted'. Workshops start with a short welcome, then the content. Students can write on paper or on a phone, and nobody needs to see what they write. The program is free, advising is one-on-one, and most advisers were first-generation students themselves. We have no research on what these students value, only advisers' impressions that family and being the first in the family at college matter a lot. Students attend about six workshops a semester, and in the fourth they bring their own budget to an adviser. Can you write a values-affirmation exercise for the start of sessions?"

## The output

```markdown
# Values-affirmation script

**People and threat:** First-generation college students in a financial-literacy program, who avoid asking questions because they feel judged about money: "I should already know how credit scores work"; "I don't want to say I can't afford what everyone else takes for granted".
**The moment:** The start of a workshop, before any financial content; and the fourth session, when students bring their own budget to an adviser.

## What the setting offers (gate)
- **Practical barrier:** None to attending: the program is free. The cost of college and pressure from family finances lie behind the embarrassment and are outside the program.
- **What people gain once they engage:** Answers to their own questions in free one-on-one advising, from advisers who were mostly first-generation students themselves (as described by the team).
- **Verdict:** Delivers.

## Value list
Unconfirmed: built from advisers' impressions. Confirm with a few current students before use.
- My family
- Being the first in my family at college
- Friendships
- Independence
- Learning new things
- My community or where I come from
- Faith
- Helping other people
- Art, music, or sport
- Humor

**Left out, and why:** Money, career success, and being good with numbers, because they are the domain under threat.

## Selection prompt
"Look at this list and choose the one value that is most important to you. Not the one you think you should choose; the one that matters most to you."

## Writing prompt (both parts)
1. "Write a few sentences about why this value is important to you."
2. "Describe a specific time when this value mattered to you or guided what you did."

**Spoken version:** Not needed; students write on paper or on a phone.

## Delivery notes
- **When:** At the first workshop, after the welcome and before any financial content.
- **Privacy:** Students keep what they write. Nobody collects or reads it.
- **How it is introduced:** "Before we start, a short piece of writing we do with every group. It is yours to keep." No mention of confidence, stress, or money.
- **Length:** About 15 minutes.
- **Repeats:** Once more, at the start of the fourth session, before students show an adviser their budget. Not at every workshop.

## What this exercise does not fix
The cost of attending college, pressure from family finances, and never having been taught how credit works. The exercise may make it easier to ask a question; whether the workshops answer it, and whether financial aid is enough, have to be judged on their own.

## The record, and what is unconfirmed here
Across 58 classroom studies, all but nine in the United States, the exercise raised the grades of students under threat slightly (g = 0.15) and did nothing for others (Wu, Spreckelsen & Cohen, 2021). A large replication in schools where it had worked before found no effect (Hanselman et al., 2017). Those studies measured grades among students facing a stereotype about their group. Asking questions in a money workshop has not been tested, and the value list has not been confirmed with students. No effect size is promised.
```
