# Evidence: Write an exercise to help people feel less judged

## Sources

- Cohen, G. L., & Sherman, D. K. (2014). The psychology of change: Self-affirmation and social psychological intervention. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 65, 333–371. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137 Supports: the mechanism. Events that threaten a person's sense of adequacy arouse stress and self-protective defenses. Writing about core personal values brings "a more expansive view of the self and its resources, weakening the implications of a threat for personal integrity". Abstract only.
- Wu, Z., Spreckelsen, T. F., & Cohen, G. L. (2021). A meta-analysis of the effect of values affirmation on academic achievement. *Journal of Social Issues*, 77(3), 702–750. https://doi.org/10.1111/josi.12415 Supports: the form of the exercise, the delivery rules, and the check of the setting. To count as a values affirmation a study had to give students a list of values and a chance to write about the one most important to them, "typically a value in a domain unrelated to school". Most studies gave it early in the year or before a stressful event, let students write in private, presented it as ungraded, and avoided calling it beneficial, which can convey "that students are in need of help". Effects were larger when it was run as a normal classroom activity, and in studies that kept close to the original materials; the authors call that last result tentative. How many times the exercise was given made no difference. The effect was largest where the achievement gap and the school's resources were both large (g = 0.36), and no different from zero where both were low. The authors conclude that the exercise should complement structural change, not replace it. Full text of the accepted manuscript.
- Hanselman, P., Rozek, C. S., Grigg, J., & Borman, G. D. (2017). New evidence on self-affirmation effects and theorized sources of heterogeneity from large-scale replications. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 109(3), 405–424. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000141 Supports: stating the record, and the length of the exercise. Seventh graders in 11 middle schools in one Midwestern US district wrote for 15 to 20 minutes about values such as family, friends, music, or sports, up to four times a year, each time before a stressful period. Teachers were asked to run it as a normal class activity and not to present it as beneficial. The first cohort had shown lasting benefits; the second showed none, and the moderators proposed in the literature did not explain the difference. Full text, in part.
- Critcher, C. R., Dunning, D., & Armor, D. A. (2010). When self-affirmations reduce defensiveness: Timing is key. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 36(7), 947–959. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167210369557 Supports: giving the exercise before the moment. In four experiments an affirmation worked only if it came before the person had begun to respond defensively. Abstract only.
- Sherman, D. K., Cohen, G. L., Nelson, L. D., Nussbaum, A. D., Bunyan, D. P., & Garcia, J. (2009). Affirmed yet unaware: Exploring the role of awareness in the process of self-affirmation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 97(5), 745–764. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015451 Supports: not announcing the purpose. Effects shrank when people were told the exercise was designed to boost self-esteem. Abstract only.
- Hall, C. C., Zhao, J., & Shafir, E. (2014). Self-affirmation among the poor. *Psychological Science*, 25(2), 619–625. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613510949 Supports: the spoken version. At an inner-city soup kitchen, where literacy was low, people described aloud an experience that made them feel successful or proud; they then did better on cognitive tasks and were more willing to take up benefits programs. Abstract only.
- Hoshino-Browne, E., Zanna, A. S., Spencer, S. J., Zanna, M. P., Kitayama, S., & Lackenbauer, S. (2005). On the cultural guises of cognitive dissonance: The case of Easterners and Westerners. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 89(3), 294–310. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.3.294 Supports: putting values held with others on the list. In one of its studies an "interdependent self-affirmation" reduced dissonance, the discomfort of having acted against one's own view, for Asian Canadians and not for European Canadians. Abstract only.
- Walton, G. M., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Wise interventions: Psychological remedies for social and personal problems. *Psychological Review*, 125(5), 617–655. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000115 Supports: the framework, in which an exercise changes the meaning people draw about themselves or their situation. Abstract only.

Three rules are this skill's own and come from no source: the second part of the writing prompt (a specific time the value mattered), the "unconfirmed" label on a value list that nobody from the group has checked, and the required statement of what the exercise does not fix.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| [Epton et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000116) | Meta-analysis, 144 tests in health; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Small positive effects on accepting a health message (d = 0.17), intentions (d = 0.14), and behavior (d = 0.32) |

| [Hanselman et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000141) | Large replication in schools; full text, in part | United States | No effect; benefits larger than 0.10 standard deviations ruled out |
| [Hadden et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12291) | Randomized trial, 562 students aged 11 to 14 in one school; abstract only | England | Three exercises over a year raised the performance of students from poorer families by 0.38 standard deviations and not that of others |
| [See et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1080/13803611.2021.2022317) | Independently evaluated randomized trial, 10,807 pupils aged 14 to 16; abstract only | England | "Positive effects for the intervention group"; pupils who completed more exercises did better. The abstract gives no size |
| [Yang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.2147/PRBM.S419112) | Field experiment, 1,534 migrant students in one secondary school; abstract only | China | Writing about core values before an English test raised scores more for students with rural registration than for those with urban registration |
| [Haire et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10461-023-04039-7) | Randomized experiment, 592 female sex workers; abstract only | Senegal | No effect on perceived risk, on taking condoms offered, or on taking an HIV test |
| [Baranov, Haushofer & Jang (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1086/702860) | Randomized trial in an informal settlement; abstract only | Kenya | Gratitude, self-affirmation, and aspirations exercises together: reported gratitude rose 0.3 standard deviations; no effect on well-being, beliefs, aspirations, or decision tasks |
| [Kizilcec et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1921417117) | Randomized interventions in 247 online courses with a quarter of a million students; abstract only | Learners from nearly every country | "Value-relevance" exercises raised completion rates in developing countries, only in courses where learners there had been completing at lower rates than others |

Outside wealthy Western countries the scan found four tests, and they disagree: a gain in one school in China, none in Senegal or in the Kenya bundle, and a gain under one condition among online learners. No review compares regions. What depends on the place is which values people hold and whether they hold them alone or with others, whether a person can write or speak in private, and whether the setting offers anything to gain once defensiveness eases.

## Replication

Mixed. An early school study in the United States found that the exercise narrowed a racial achievement gap by 40% (Cohen, Garcia, Apfel, & Master, 2006, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1128317; abstract only). A replication in the same district, with the same procedures, found nothing, and the moderators proposed in the literature did not explain why. A reanalysis of a 2010 study of the gender gap in science found that its data showed no improvement for women (Serra-Garcia, Hansen, & Gneezy, 2020, https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620923587; abstract only). The meta-analysis of classroom studies finds a small average benefit for students under threat, with wide variation.

## What to check locally

1. Which values people in this group hold, asked of someone from the group, and whether any value on the list carries the stigma.
2. Whether a person can write or speak without being overheard, and who could see what they wrote.
3. Whether people feel judged at the moment chosen; the exercise did nothing for people who were not under threat.
4. Whether the setting offers something to gain once people engage, and whether a practical barrier remains.
5. Whether anyone in the group is in distress that recalling a personal memory could worsen.
