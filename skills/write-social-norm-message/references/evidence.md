# Evidence: Tell people what most of their peers already do

## Sources

- The Behavioural Insights Team (2024). *EAST: Four simple ways to apply behavioural insights* (revised and updated edition). https://bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/BIT-EAST.pdf Supports: the rules for choosing and wording the message. Section 3.1, read in full, says that telling people what most others do can shift behavior, that "the greatest impact can come from saying both what people do and what is approved", that norms work best when the recipient feels a connection to the group and can backfire when the group is one the recipient dislikes, that people doing better than average may move toward it, that emphasizing how common an unwanted behavior is can increase it, and that for voluntary contributions to a common good, such as giving blood, saying most people give can lead others to leave it to them. On what to do when most people do not do the behavior, it says trials of trend messages disagree.
- Schultz, P. W., Nolan, J. M., Cialdini, R. B., Goldstein, N. J., & Griskevicius, V. (2007). The constructive, destructive, and reconstructive power of social norms. *Psychological Science*, 18(5), 429–434. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x Supports: the line of approval and the separate check on people who already do the behavior. A message giving average neighborhood energy use produced savings or a rise depending on whether a household used more or less than average; adding a message of approval or disapproval removed the rise. Abstract only.
- Cialdini, R. B., Demaine, L. J., Sagarin, B. J., Barrett, D. W., Rhoads, K., & Winter, P. L. (2006). Managing social norms for persuasive impact. *Social Influence*, 1(1), 3–15. https://doi.org/10.1080/15534510500181459 Supports: never stating how common the unwanted behavior is. At Arizona's Petrified Forest National Park, signs focused on how much others stole were most likely to increase theft, and signs focused on others' disapproval were most likely to suppress it. Abstract only.
- Kettle, S., Hernandez, M., Ruda, S., & Sanders, M. (2016). *Behavioral interventions in tax compliance: Evidence from Guatemala*. World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 7690. https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-7690 Supports: stating the true share plainly. Abstract only.
- Allcott, H. (2011). Social norms and energy conservation. *Journal of Public Economics*, 95(9–10), 1082–1095. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2011.03.003 Supports: the replication note. Letters comparing a household's electricity use with its neighbors', tested with 600,000 households, cut use by 2.0% on average, by 6.3% in the highest-using tenth and by 0.3% in the lowest. Different categories of approval wording "played an insignificant role" in keeping low users from increasing. Abstract only.
- Hallsworth, M., List, J. A., Metcalfe, R. D., & Vlaev, I. (2017). The behavioralist as tax collector: Using natural field experiments to enhance tax compliance. *Journal of Public Economics*, 148, 14–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2017.02.003 Supports: leading with what people do. In tax reminder letters, statements of what others do had a significantly larger effect on payment than statements of what others think should be done. Working-paper version read (NBER Working Paper 20007).
- Bursztyn, L., González, A. L., & Yanagizawa-Drott, D. (2020). Misperceived social norms: Women working outside the home in Saudi Arabia. *American Economic Review*, 110(10), 2997–3029. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20180975 Supports: stating what most approve of when people underestimate it. Abstract only.

The rule that a statement of what most people do needs at least half of the named group is this skill's own line. The sources give the warning; they do not give a threshold.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Rhodes, Shulman & McClaran (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqz023) | Meta-analysis of 110 articles on norm appeals; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Consistent support across outcomes, with small effects and significant variation between studies. Messages about what others approve of may be more effective than had been thought |
| [Bergquist, Nilsson & Schultz (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2019.101941) | Meta-analysis of 91 field experiments, 227,730 participants, on environmental behavior; highlights only | Not listed in the highlights | A positive effect against no-treatment controls. Norms tended to be more influential in individualistic countries than in collectivistic ones |
| [Kettle et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-7690) | Randomized field experiment, 43,387 taxpayers; abstract only | Guatemala | A letter citing the 64.5% of taxpayers who had already paid was one of two letters that raised the rate of payment and the amount paid. The effect remained at 12 months |
| [Del Carpio (2013)](https://www.econ.ku.dk/Kalender/seminarer/18022014/Carpio.Are_the_neighbors_cheating_Nov12.pdf) | Randomized field experiment in two municipalities of Lima; working paper, abstract only | Peru | An official letter stating the average rate of property-tax compliance raised compliance by 20% relative to the control group; a plain reminder raised it by 10%. The norm letter also raised what people believed about enforcement |
| [Bursztyn, González & Yanagizawa-Drott (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20180975) | Randomized experiments with young married men, and with women; abstract only | Saudi Arabia | Most men privately supported women working outside the home and underestimated other men's support. Men told the true level were more willing to help their wives look for work, and months later their wives were more likely to have applied and interviewed for a job |
| [Datta et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-7283) | Randomized trial of stickers on water bills; abstract only | Costa Rica | A comparison with the neighborhood cut water use by 3.7% to 5.6%, mostly among high-consumption households. This is a comparison of each household with its peers, not a single message |
| [Jaime Torres & Carlsson (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeem.2018.08.005) | Randomized field experiment with monthly consumption reports; abstract only | Colombia | Reports with normative messages cut water use by up to 6.8% in targeted households, and by 5.8% in the first six months among households not targeted. Also a comparison report |

Outside wealthy Western countries the field trials of a single message are about tax, and each reported a favorable majority figure sent by an official body. The scan found no field trial of a norm message for a health or farming behavior in a low- or middle-income country that compared it with the same message without the norm line. What depends on the place is who counts as a peer, whether a figure from this sender is believed, and whether the audience reads a majority figure as information or as pressure.

## Replication

The direction replicates and the effects are small. The two findings behind the warnings are narrower. The rise among people who already do well was shown in one study of household energy use, and a far larger program of the same kind in the United States did not see it: the lowest users still cut use slightly, and approval wording made no measurable difference to them. The increase in an unwanted behavior after describing how common it is comes from one field study in one park. Both warnings cost almost nothing to heed, which is why the skill keeps them. Whether a message about a rising trend works when most people do not yet do the behavior is unsettled; a study in the United States found it changed food orders at one café ([Sparkman & Walton, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617719950), abstract only), and the Behavioural Insights Team reports that other studies found no effect.

## What to check locally

1. Who the audience counts as "people like me" for this behavior, which may not be the unit the data come in.
2. Whether the share can be computed for that group from records, and how old the records are.
3. Whether a figure from this sender is believed, and whether the sender's approval is welcome.
4. Whether people who already do the behavior can be told apart from the rest before the message goes out.
5. Whether the behavior is under-reported in surveys, which would make both the true share and the belief about it unreliable.
