# Evidence: Estimate the cost per unit of behavior change

## Sources

- Dhaliwal, I., Duflo, E., Glennerster, R., & Tulloch, C. (2013). Comparative cost-effectiveness analysis to inform policy in developing countries. In *Education Policy in Developing Countries* (pp. 285–338). University of Chicago Press. https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226078854.003.0008 Supports: the ingredients list, because budget information "tends to be incomplete" and "specifying all the ingredients necessary to replicate the program and then gathering unit cost information helps to ensure that a complete picture of the program's costs is included and guarantees comparability"; pricing free goods and services at market cost, since inputs free in a small project may not be free at scale; transfers, which "represent an accounting cost to the government or organization undertaking the program, but not to the society as a whole", so the chapter discusses both treatments; labeling a precisely estimated zero "no significant impact" instead of computing a ratio, and leaving out imprecise zeros; and choosing assumptions to fit the perspective of the intended user. The chapter takes the ingredients method from Levin and McEwan (2001). Full text of the authors' December 2012 manuscript, https://www.povertyactionlab.org/publication/cost-effectiveness.
- Bhula, R., Mahoney, M., & Murphy, K. (2020). *Conducting cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)*. J-PAL Research Resources. https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/conducting-cost-effectiveness-analysis-cea Supports: the defaults. CEA needs "two pieces of data: an estimate of the program's impact and the cost of the program"; J-PAL's analyses include "only the incremental cost of adding a new education program"; local currencies are converted "using standard (not PPP) exchange rates"; "a ten percent discount rate is applied for costs incurred over multiple years"; goods received free are counted where free supply would not be guaranteed elsewhere; costs include "the monetary value of both cash and in-kind transfers"; and results are reported per US$100 spent.
- Banerjee, A. V., Duflo, E., Glennerster, R., & Kothari, D. (2010). Improving immunisation coverage in rural India: clustered randomised controlled evaluation of immunisation campaigns with and without incentives. *BMJ*, 340, c2220. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.c2220 Supports: the fixed-cost check and the transfers line. Full immunization reached 39% of children in villages with reliable camps plus small incentives, 18% with camps alone, and 6% in control villages. The cost to the implementer of fully immunizing a child was $27.94 with incentives and $55.83 without, because camps ran fixed hours "regardless of the number of children present" and the nurse's pay was spread over more children. The authors add that the lentils given as incentives were a cost to the implementer, but "it is not clear they should be considered as a cost". Full text.
- Skivington, K., Matthews, L., Simpson, S. A., et al. (2021). A new framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions: update of Medical Research Council guidance. *BMJ*, 374, n2061. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n2061 Supports: economic evaluation as "the comparative analysis of alternative courses of action in terms of both costs (resource use) and consequences (outcomes, effects)"; the point that broader approaches, such as cost-benefit or cost-consequence analysis, "will often be more suitable" for complex interventions than a single cost-effectiveness ratio; and the warning that those who pay and those who benefit may differ. Full text.
- Glennerster, R., & Takavarasha, K. (2013). *Running Randomized Evaluations: A Practical Guide*. Princeton University Press. http://runningres.com/ Supports: comparing programs by cost-effectiveness as part of drawing policy lessons from evaluations (chapter 9). Read from the book's companion site.
- World Health Organization & ExpandNet (2011). *Beginning with the end in mind*. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241502320 Supports: costing participants' own costs. A project team should "determine the costs of implementation and costs to users". Full text.
- Levin, H. M., & Belfield, C. (2015). Guiding the development and use of cost-effectiveness analysis in education. *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness*, 8(3), 400–418. https://doi.org/10.1080/19345747.2014.915604 Supports: the warning against costing from the budget. When cost-effectiveness analysis is used in education, "it often fails to meet methodological standards, especially with regard to cost measurement". Abstract only.

The method does not depend on the population. The prices do: wages for volunteer and participant time, exchange rates, and whether inputs that were free in the pilot would stay free.

## Replication

Not applicable; this is a costing method. Its sources disagree on one choice: J-PAL's analyses count transfers as a cost, while the Dhaliwal chapter notes they are not a cost to society, and the Rajasthan trial's authors doubt the incentives should count. The skill shows the ratio both ways for that reason.

## What to check locally

1. Which inputs the program used without paying for them, and the local wage or rental value of each.
2. Whether the effect is this program's own estimate or a planning estimate.
3. How the comparator programs were costed: perspective, transfers, currency year, and whether staff time was priced.
4. The funder's conventions for currency year, exchange rate, and discount rate.
5. The share of delivery cost that is fixed and would fall per person as the program grows.
6. Whether the outcome was measured the same way as the comparator's.
