# Evidence: Count the hassle between intention and completion

## Sources

- Sunstein, C. R. (2022). Sludge audits. *Behavioural Public Policy*, 6(4), 654–673. https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2019.32 (first published online in 2020). Supports: the audit itself. Sludge is "excessive or unjustified frictions, such as paperwork burdens, that cost time or money"; institutions "should regularly conduct Sludge Audits to catalogue the costs of sludge and to decide when and how to reduce it". A policy argument with no trial of the method. Abstract only.
- The Behavioural Insights Team (2024). *EAST: Four simple ways to apply behavioural insights* (revised and updated edition). https://bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/BIT-EAST.pdf Supports: logging practical and mental effort, and the "keep" tag. Section 1.2, "Reduce the effort required": "Every extra step or moment of effort required may reduce the likelihood that people will act". It reports that governments "have started running 'sludge audits' to identify and reduce these burdens", that frictions "tend to harm those who need help the most", and that "increasing frictions can be in a person's best interest", as with cooling-off periods.
- Datta, S., & Mullainathan, S. (2012). *Behavioral Design: A New Approach to Development Policy*. CGD Policy Paper 016. https://www.cgdev.org/sites/default/files/Datta_Mullainathan_Behavioral_Design.pdf Supports: counting small steps. Principle 3, "Remove Snags to Choosing": "seemingly trivial steps, decisions and choices (a form to fill or the need to submit one that has already been filled, for example) can drastically reduce the number of people who participate in a program."
- OECD (2019). *Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit*. https://doi.org/10.1787/9ea76a8f-en Supports: where to look. "Work with friction" advises reducing "the number of actions, clicks or questions" a person must get through, starting from the flow chart of the behavior.
- World Bank (2015). *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-0342-0 Supports: going through the process as a user. Chapter 11 suggests that policy designers "try to sign up for their own programs or access existing services to diagnose problems firsthand", and its table 11.2 lists "channel and hassle factors" among designs with experimental evidence.
- Bettinger, E. P., Long, B. T., Oreopoulos, P., & Sanbonmatsu, L. (2012). The role of application assistance and information in college decisions: Results from the H&R Block FAFSA experiment. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 127(3), 1205–1242. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs017 Supports: ranking help above information. Among low-income families in the United States, high school seniors whose parents got help completing the student-aid form were 8 percentage points more likely to have completed two years of college (36% against 28%). Families given information without help "did not experience improved outcomes". Abstract only.

The three tags, the "check" tag, and the order of changes within the program's control are this skill's own.

## Where it has been tested

Searched November 2026.

| Source | Type | Countries | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Halling & Baekgaard (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/muad023) | Systematic review of 119 articles and working papers; full text | All six inhabited continents; 82% of studies in the United States, Europe, or Australia | Research supports the claims that burdens have consequences and fall unevenly on different groups. No study compares burdens across countries |
| [Bendtsen (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.70085) | Meta-analysis of 51 field experiments, 187 effects; abstract only | Not stated in the abstract | Help that reduced the work of complying raised benefit take-up by 8.31 percentage points on average; information raised it by 3.39. Effects on applications were about twice the effects on benefits received |
| [Devoto, Duflo, Dupas, Parienté & Pons (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.4.4.68) | Randomized field experiment, 845 households; working-paper version read in full | Morocco | Households told about a water connection on credit, whose paperwork was photographed at home and approved in bulk, bought a connection in 69% of cases within six months, against 10% of households left to apply by themselves. Information and help came together, so their effects cannot be separated |
| [Duflo, Kremer & Robinson (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.6.2350) | Field experiment with maize farmers; abstract only | Kenya | Farmers who passed up profitable fertilizer bought it when offered free delivery for a limited time just after harvest. The authors model the barrier as a small fixed cost of buying, combined with procrastination |
| [Alatas, Banerjee, Hanna, Olken, Purnamasari & Wai-Poi (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1086/685299) | Village-level randomized experiment; abstract only | Indonesia | Requiring people to apply for a cash transfer produced "substantially poorer beneficiaries" than automatic enrollment with the same asset test, mainly because the non-poor did not apply. Raising the application cost further did not improve targeting |
| [Heinrich (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/muv034) | Observational analysis of evaluation data; abstract only | South Africa | 60% of Child Support Grant recipients had an interruption in payments that appears linked to administrative burden, and 80% of those stoppages were in error |
| [Deshpande & Li (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180076) | Natural experiment from field-office closings; abstract only | United States | Closings cut the number of disability recipients nearby by 16%, most among people with moderately severe conditions and low education |

None found: a study in any country that compares programs that ran a friction audit with programs that did not. An OECD paper, [*Fixing frictions: 'sludge audits' around the world* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1787/5e9bb35c-en), draws good-practice principles from ten government case studies; its abstract reports no effects and names no countries. Searches covered systematic reviews of administrative burden, take-up experiments in developing countries, sludge audits by governments, application requirements in targeting, and administrative burden in South Africa.

What depends on the place is who a step turns away. In the United States study the people lost were the less educated; in Indonesia they were the better-off. The cost of a trip, the documents people hold, and who has the authority to waive a step also differ.

## Replication

The direction holds across many field experiments: fewer or easier steps mean more people finish, and help with the steps does more than information about them. Sizes vary widely and are smaller for benefits received than for applications made. Whether friction falls hardest on the worst-off differs between studies: a United States study found that it did, and the Indonesian experiment found the opposite for an application requirement. The audit as a method has no evaluation.

## What to check locally

1. Which steps the law, a regulator, or a partner's rules require, and who can confirm it.
2. What a trip costs here in fares, lost earnings, and childcare; the official process never records it.
3. Whether the steps were observed under the user's conditions: their phone, their documents, their working hours, their language.
4. Who is lost at each step, and whether the program means to serve them.
5. Whether a count per step exists; if not, whether a two-week tally at each place is cheaper than guessing.
