# Evidence: Adapt a growth-mindset lesson to a local school

## Sources

- Walton, G. M., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Wise interventions: Psychological remedies for social and personal problems. *Psychological Review*, 125(5), 617–655. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000115 Supports: the framework, in which an exercise changes the meaning people draw about themselves or their situation, and an early change can become embedded. Abstract only.
- Yeager, D. S., Hanselman, P., Walton, G. M., Murray, J. S., Crosnoe, R., Muller, C., et al. (2019). A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement. *Nature*, 573, 364–369. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y Supports: how the lesson is built, and the check of the school. Two online sessions of about 25 minutes, some 20 days apart, taught that ability develops through effort, challenging work, better learning strategies, and asking for help, with the image of the brain as a muscle that grows stronger with use. Students then taught the idea to a future ninth grader who was struggling. They "were not told outright that they should work hard or employ particular study or learning strategies". Among 6,320 lower-achieving students in 65 schools, grades rose by 0.10 points (95% CI 0.04 to 0.16), and more where peers favored taking on challenges; that comparison is between schools as they were, not an experiment. The analysis was pre-registered. Full text.
- Yeager, D. S., Carroll, J. M., Buontempo, J., Cimpian, A., Woody, S., Crosnoe, R., et al. (2022). Teacher mindsets help explain where a growth-mindset intervention does and doesn't work. *Psychological Science*, 33(1), 18–32. https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976211028984 Supports: asking what teachers say and do. In 9,167 student records matched with 223 math teachers from the same study, the lesson's effect depended on teachers who themselves believed ability can grow. Abstract only.
- Yeager, D. S., Walton, G. M., Brady, S. T., Akcinar, E. N., Paunesku, D., Keane, L., et al. (2016). Teaching a lay theory before college narrows achievement gaps at scale. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 113(24). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1524360113 Supports: the advice task. The paper names the technique "saying-is-believing" and says it "allows students to personalize generic materials, promoting internalization". Full text.

A systematic review and meta-analysis with recommendations for best practices. *Psychological Bulletin*, 149(3–4), 133–173. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352 Supports: stating the record and promising no effect. Abstract only.
- Burnette, J. L., Billingsley, J., Banks, G. C., Knouse, L. E., Hoyt, C. L., Pollack, J. M., & Simon, S. (2023). No review restricted to low- and middle-income countries was found. The trials outside wealthy Western countries disagree: small gains in Peru and Indonesia, none in Argentina, changed beliefs without changed scores in China. Searches for trials in India, South Africa, and Kenya found none with results. What depends on the place is whether the school backs the message: a system where one exam decides and marked work is never returned does not support "you can improve".

## Replication

Contested. The largest pre-registered trial found a small gain in grades among lower-achieving students in the United States, where peers and teachers supported the message. Two meta-analyses published side by side in 2023 disagree: one finds an average effect near zero that it attributes to weak study design and bias, the other a small effect among students expected to benefit. The Argentina trial used the same parts as the tested lesson, a reading and a letter, at scale, and found nothing.

## What to check locally

1. Whether students read a poor result as a verdict on fixed ability, in their own words.
2. Whether a poor result is followed by feedback or a second attempt, as a fact and not a plan.
3. What teachers say when they hand results back, and what peers say about students who try hard.
4. Whether "ability grows" is credible where one examination decides a student's track.
5. Whether a written letter suits the reading level, or a spoken version is needed.
